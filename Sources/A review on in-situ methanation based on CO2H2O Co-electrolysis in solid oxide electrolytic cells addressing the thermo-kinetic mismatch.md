## 1\. Introduction

Fossil resources are being depleted at an accelerating rate, and carbon-based energy systems are imposing a heavier environmental burden; together, these pressures have pushed renewable energy technologies to the center of attention. Renewable electricity is widely seen as cleaner and more sustainable, yet large-scale deployment still runs into familiar obstacles: Its regionality, intermittency, and dynamism. For that reason, converting renewable electricity into chemical fuels has become a key route for both energy storage and carbon utilization.

Among the available conversion technologies, solid oxide electrolysis cells (SOECs) have drawn particular interest \[,,,,, \]. The reason is straightforward: they offer high energy-conversion efficiency, strong fuel flexibility, and the ability to turn both electrical and thermal energy into chemical energy in the form of H <sub>2</sub>, CO, syngas, and even hydrocarbons. Viewed through the Power-to-X framework, CO <sub>2</sub> /H <sub>2</sub> O co-electrolysis in SOECs is especially attractive because it provides a direct pathway for converting captured CO <sub>2</sub> and renewable electricity into carbon-containing fuels. In oxygen-ion-conducting SOECs (), CO <sub>2</sub> and H <sub>2</sub> O enter the fuel electrode, where they are electrochemically reduced to CO and H <sub>2</sub>; at the same time, O <sup>2−</sup> ions migrate through the dense electrolyte and release oxygen at the oxygen electrode. Under these high-temperature conditions, electrochemical reduction is accompanied by rapid heterogeneous reactions, resulting in a strongly coupled reaction environment involving charge transfer, surface catalysis, and mass transport.

Among the possible products, methane is of particular interest due to its high volumetric energy density, compatibility with existing natural gas infrastructure, and strategic value for long-duration and seasonal energy storage. Methane synthesis based on SOEC co-electrolysis can proceed through two distinct routes (). For two-step thermally decoupled route, the SOEC mainly serves as a source of H <sub>2</sub> or syngas, which is subsequently converted to methane in a separate methanation reactor. This route has the potential to reduce the high electricity demand of electrolysis by recovering waste heat from the exothermic methanation reaction. In contrast, the one-step thermally coupled route produces methane in situ within the SOEC reactor, thereby eliminating the need for a downstream methanation unit and creating the possibility of tighter thermal integration, higher overall efficiency, and more compact system design \[,, \]. This review focuses on the latter one, namely SOEC in-situ methanation.

![](https://ars.els-cdn.com/content/image/1-s2.0-S2772970226000143-gr2.jpg)

Depending on the electrolyte type, SOECs can be classified as proton-conducting SOECs (H-SOECs) and oxygen-ion-conducting SOECs (O-SOECs). H-SOECs offer advantages at relatively lower temperatures because of the lower activation energy and higher conductivity of proton transport, which is potentially favorable for methane synthesis \[,\]. However, uncertainties in CO <sub>2</sub> conversion pathways, limited Faradaic efficiency and anode stability during co-electrolysis, and the insufficient chemical stability of proton-conducting electrolytes under CO <sub>2</sub> -containing conditions have so far restricted their development \[,, \]. Consequently, most current studies on direct methane synthesis are still centered on O-SOEC systems.

The development of SOEC in-situ methanation has progressed steadily from early proof-of-concept studies to the substantial methane yields achieved today \[,,,, \]. And the SOEC in-situ methanation is not simply the sequential combination of electrolysis and methanation, but the outcome of a strongly coupled network involving H <sub>2</sub> O electrolysis, possible direct CO <sub>2</sub> electroreduction, reverse water-gas shift (RWGS), thermal catalytic hydrogenation of CO and CO <sub>2</sub>, and side reactions such as carbon deposition \[,,,,, \] ().

A central challenge arising from in-situ methanation based on CO <sub>2</sub> /H <sub>2</sub> O co-electrolysis in SOEC is the thermo-kinetic mismatch between the two main functionalities of the process. In SOECs, the total energy input required for electrolysis (ΔH) remains nearly unchanged with increasing temperature, while the electrical energy demand (ΔG) decreases and the thermal energy requirement increases accordingly. This behavior is observed for both H <sub>2</sub> O and CO <sub>2</sub> electrolysis (a), and is more pronounced in CO <sub>2</sub> /H <sub>2</sub> O co-electrolysis (b), where the reduction in electrical energy is approximately compensated by the increase in thermal energy. Given that thermal energy is generally less costly than electrical energy, high-temperature operation is thermodynamically favorable for CO <sub>2</sub> /H <sub>2</sub> O co-electrolysis. Moreover, elevated temperature lowers ohmic resistance and promotes electrode reaction kinetics. Therefore, high-temperature SOEC operation is generally considered advantageous for CO <sub>2</sub> /H <sub>2</sub> O co-electrolysis in terms of both energy efficiency and operating cost. The curves shown in c illustrate the relationship between ΔG and ΔH for the water gas shift reaction (WGS). Notably, at 816 °C, the ΔG value for the WGS reaction reaches zero, indicating the WGS reaction's preference at lower temperatures (ΔG<0), while the opposite holds true for the reverse process (RWGS). In contrast, according to the thermodynamic diagram of CO methanation (), the CH <sub>4</sub> yield reaches nearly 100% at pressures of 1 atm or higher and temperatures below 250 °C, and the optimal temperature range for the reaction, considering both thermodynamic and kinetic aspects, falls between 250 °C and 350 °C. CO <sub>2</sub> methanation can achieve nearly 100% CH <sub>4</sub> selectivity when the pressure is maintained at 1 atm or above and the temperature is below 400 °C. Compared with CO methanation under similar temperature and pressure conditions, however, CO <sub>2</sub> methanation is generally more difficult, mainly because CO <sub>2</sub> is a highly stable molecule and its activation requires more demanding catalytic conditions. As shown in, the favorable temperature window for CO <sub>2</sub> methanation is typically located between 250 °C and 400 °C. This temperature preference raises an important question for SOEC-based in-situ methanation: how can a process that benefits from high-temperature electrolysis also maintain conditions suitable for methane formation? At temperatures above 700 °C, the enhanced electrolysis kinetics and intensified RWGS activity tend to drive the system toward syngas production rather than CH <sub>4</sub> formation \[,\]. When the temperature decreases below 400 °C, the equilibrium shifts toward methane \[\], but the SOEC may suffer from increased ohmic resistance and polarization losses. A more realistic strategy is therefore to operate, whenever possible, in an intermediate temperature range of approximately 500–650 °C \[,, \]. When medium-temperature operation is not feasible, spatial separation of electrolysis and methanation zones becomes essential.

To address this limitation, two main reactor-operation modes have been explored \[\] (). In the single-temperature-zone mode, the cathode itself acts as the methanation-active interface or is directly coupled with a catalyst layer \[\]. In the two-temperature-zone mode, electrolysis and methanation are spatially separated, and methane is formed in a cooler catalyst bed located near or downstream of the electrode \[,\]. In addition to temperature, methane yield is strongly influenced by pressure, applied potential, feed composition, and flow configuration, indicating that SOEC in-situ methanation is governed by the coupled effects of materials, operating conditions, and reactor architecture \[,,,,, \].

![](https://ars.els-cdn.com/content/image/1-s2.0-S2772970226000143-gr7.jpg)

Long-term durability, however, remains one of the main barriers that must be overcome before SOEC in-situ methanation can move toward practical deployment. In recent years, considerable progress has been made in improving electrode activity, electrolyte conductivity, and interfacial stability, yet the degradation rates reported for even relatively stable SOEC stacks are still higher than those required for commercial operation \[\]. Despite these unresolved durability issues, industrial and engineering interest in SOEC-based methane synthesis is increasing. Recent demonstrations of high-efficiency methane production under hydrogen-rich conditions, modular reactor concepts with scalable output, and dedicated commercialization-oriented development programs all suggest that the technology is no longer limited to proof-of-concept studies \[,\].

Against this background, a systematic review of SOEC in-situ methanation is both necessary and timely. Current research is progressing along several closely connected directions, including the clarification of reaction pathways, the design of multifunctional materials that can support both CO <sub>2</sub> /H <sub>2</sub> O co-electrolysis and methane formation, and reactor engineering strategies aimed at alleviating the thermo-kinetic mismatch through local control of temperature, flow field, and reactive atmosphere \[\]. Following this logic, this review first discusses the reaction mechanisms involved in CO <sub>2</sub> /H <sub>2</sub> O co-electrolysis and methane formation in SOECs. It then examines material design strategies for direct methane synthesis, with particular attention to cathode functionality and electrolyte/electrode compatibility. Reactor design approaches for improving thermo-electrical coupling are subsequently analyzed, before the review concludes with remaining challenges and future perspectives.

## 2\. Reaction mechanisms of direct methane synthesis via CO2/H2O co-electrolysis in SOEC

The working principle outlined above reveals the intricate reaction and transfer processes involved in the SOECs during the CO <sub>2</sub> /H <sub>2</sub> O co-electrolysis reaction. At the fuel electrode's triple-phase boundary (TPB), a charge transfer reaction occurs, releasing ions that are subsequently transported via the ion conductor to the oxygen electrode. Concurrently, heterogeneous catalytic reactions, including the RWGS, methanation, and carbon deposition, take place on the surface of the nickel metal conductor. These reactions, along with the bulk diffusion processes within the porous electrode, collectively influence the electrochemical performance of the SOEC's porous fuel electrode.

The lack of adequate in situ techniques at elevated operating temperatures (500–1000 °C) hinders direct observation of surface reactions \[,\]. Nevertheless, a combination of experimental and computational studies has enabled the proposal of several mechanistic pathways. This chapter first describes the individual reduction mechanisms of CO <sub>2</sub> and H <sub>2</sub> O, methane formation, then discusses the coupling reactions, and finally integrates these into a comprehensive reaction network for in-situ methane formation.

Limited insights exist regarding the electrolysis mechanism of CO <sub>2</sub> on the fuel side. Although the electrochemical reduction of CO <sub>2</sub> in SOECs occurs on different cathode materials, several common mechanistic features can be identified ().

In conventional Ni-YSZ electrodes, the adsorbed CO <sub>2</sub> species are activated mainly on metallic Ni sites, where direct electron transfer facilitates C=O bond cleavage and ultimately leads to CO formation (a), and this pathway is relatively straightforward. The situation becomes more complex when oxide cathodes are involved. For perovskite-type materials such as LSF, spectroscopic and computational studies suggest that CO <sub>2</sub> activation often proceeds through carbonate-like CO <sub>3</sub> <sup>2−</sup> intermediates (b). Such intermediates indicate a strong interaction between CO <sub>2</sub> molecules and surface lattice oxygen, suggesting that surface oxygen species participate directly in bond activation rather than simply providing a passive structural environment. Ceria-based electrodes, represented by SDC, introduce another layer of complexity. In these systems, the Ce <sup>4+</sup> /Ce <sup>3+</sup> redox couple and the abundance of oxygen vacancies strongly affect CO <sub>2</sub> adsorption, activation, and subsequent conversion. CO <sub>2</sub> reduction is therefore not controlled by electron transfer alone; it also depends on vacancy formation and migration, as well as the stability of carbonate-type surface species (c). From a practical viewpoint, this coupling between redox chemistry and defect chemistry is particularly relevant because it influences both catalytic activity and resistance to carbon-related degradation. Skafte et al. \[\] further identified an inhibition mechanism on ceria, providing useful evidence that the surface properties of ceria and other vacancy-rich oxides can be tuned to improve carbon tolerance. As a result, doped ceria has become a model system for probing the mechanistic complexity of practical electrochemical reactions.

	Methane formation

During co-electrolysis in SOECs, the generated intermediates—primarily H <sub>2</sub> and CO—serve directly as precursors for subsequent methanation reactions. Hydrogen participates in catalytic hydrogenation of CO and CO <sub>2</sub>, while CO can be further converted either through CO methanation or through RWGS to regenerate CO <sub>2</sub> as a reactant. Notebly, In the SOEC co-electrolysis of CO <sub>2</sub> /H <sub>2</sub> O, the carbon monoxide generated in situ dissociates and accumulates on the cathode surface with increasing applied voltage. Deposited carbon directly participates in the electrochemical reaction of TPB during the direct synthesis of methane via SOEC \[\]:

Although deposited carbon plays a direct role in the production of methane through SOEC co-electrolysis \[\], its role in SOEC systems must be carefully distinguished from other electrocatalytic and thermocatalytic pathways. Carbon deposition, rather than being a constructive mechanism, is primarily regarded as a degradation pathway that adversely affects electrode stability and performance. Excessive carbon growth, resulting from undesirable CO disproportionation or deep reduction of CO <sub>2</sub>, can lead to catalyst deactivation, microstructural damage, and performance loss under typical SOEC operating conditions.
- •
	Coupled reaction mechanisms: RWGS and Non-Faradaic Electrochemical Modification of Catalytic Activity (NEMCA) effect

In the CO <sub>2</sub> /H <sub>2</sub> O co-electrolysis process of SOEC, the RWGS reaction is a significant influencing factor, rendering the mechanism of SOEC co-electrolysis more intricate. Some research indicates that CO <sub>2</sub> is predominantly transformed through RWGS due to the convergence of the polarization curves of H <sub>2</sub> O electrolysis and co-electrolysis, along with comparable area-specific resistances (ASRs) (a) \[,, \]. By contrast, other studies suggest that electrolysis and RWGS occur simultaneously during co-electrolysis. Ebbesen et al. \[\], for example, reported polarization curves and impedance spectra for CO <sub>2</sub> /H <sub>2</sub> O co-electrolysis that differed clearly from those obtained for H <sub>2</sub> O or CO <sub>2</sub> electrolysis alone, implying that both H <sub>2</sub> O and CO <sub>2</sub> participate directly in the electrochemical process (b). Accordingly, the dominant pathway for CO formation remains controversial: CO may arise mainly from direct CO <sub>2</sub> electrolysis (R1) or from the RWGS reaction (R2). These inconsistent conclusions are likely related to the strong sensitivity of the co-electrolysis mechanism to experimental conditions ().

Besides operating parameters, the influence of the NEMCA effect on in-situ methanation via SOEC is also significant \[\]. Under typical operating conditions, SOEC supply reaction ions (O <sup>2−</sup> or H <sup>+</sup>) via the Faraday process, where the reaction rate is generally proportional to the applied current or the ion transport rate. When a potential difference is applied, O <sup>2−</sup> migrates from the solid electrolyte to the catalyst surface, resulting in the formation of an electrochemical double layer. This double electric layer modifies the electron density and chemical state of the catalyst surface, thereby affecting the adsorption and activation of reactants and influencing catalytic activity. 	Integrated reaction pathway for in-situ methanation
	Based on the above discussion, a typical reaction network for in-situ methane synthesis via CO <sub>2</sub> /H <sub>2</sub> O co-electrolysis in SOECs can be summarized as comprising four key steps:
- (1)
	Due to a lower reaction barrier of H <sub>2</sub> O compared to CO <sub>2</sub>, H <sub>2</sub> O electrolysis tends to prevail initially during co-electrolysis.
- (2)
	As more electrical energy is supplied, CO <sub>2</sub> electrolysis necessitates a higher reaction barrier for initiation, leading to simultaneous H <sub>2</sub> O electrolysis alongside CO <sub>2</sub> through CO <sub>2</sub> /H <sub>2</sub> O co-electrolysis \[,,\]. While a portion being reduced to CO at the electrode surface, another portion of the CO <sub>2</sub> that has dissociated and adsorbed onto the electrode surface undergoes hydrogenation to form CH <sub>4</sub> (methanation) or CO (RWGS).
- (3)
	Hydrogenation of CO generated in situ (through co-electrolysis and RWGS) to form CH <sub>4</sub>.
- (4)
	Electro-reduction of water vapor produced during in-situ methanation prevents dissociation of methane through water vapor reforming \[\].

Luo et al. \[\] proposed the mechanism for in situ methanation in a two-temperature-zone SOEC:
- (1)
	Cathode surface in the high-temperature region: acts as an inlet gas-control region where H <sub>2</sub> reacts with CO <sub>2</sub> to produce CH <sub>4</sub>.
- (2)
	Positive electrode–electrolyte interface: serves as the electrochemical-control region where electrochemically produced H <sub>2</sub> /CO rapidly form CH <sub>4</sub>.
- (3)
	Cathode surface in the low-temperature region: functions as a temperature-controlled zone where the methanation equilibrium shifts positively, facilitated by CO <sub>2</sub> /CO methanation over a non-homogeneous catalyst.

In conclusion, there is still room for improvement of the intrinsic mechanisms of SOEC co-electrolysis and in-situ methanation. Mechanistic studies of CO <sub>2</sub> /H <sub>2</sub> O co-electrolysis reveal that while direct CO <sub>2</sub> reduction and RWGS are highly efficient at higher temperatures, methane formation is increasingly constrained by thermodynamics above 400 °C. This disconnect constitutes the root cause of the thermo-kinetic mismatch. Addressing this issue requires engineering solutions that optimize reaction pathways within a unified temperature range, which will be detailed in the following sections on material development strategies.

## 3\. Design strategies for the materials of SOEC in-situ methanation

### 3.1. Cathodes (fuel electrodes): negotiating the thermo-kinetic mismatch in SOEC in-situ methanation

As discussed above, in-situ methanation in SOECs is not governed by a single elementary reaction, but by a strongly coupled network involving CO <sub>2</sub> /H <sub>2</sub> O co-electrolysis, RWGS, CO/CO <sub>2</sub> hydrogenation, and electrochemical promotion effects. The relative importance of these pathways varies significantly with the operating temperature, gas composition, current density, and local reaction environment near the cathode. In particular, the thermo-kinetic mismatch originates from an intrinsic conflict between electrolysis and methanation, and the key to bridging this mismatch lies in the development of multifunctional materials that can maintain sufficient electrochemical activity at intermediate or elevated temperatures while also enabling methanation to proceed within an expanded or shifted temperature window.

#### 3.1.1. Traditional electrolytic optimization materials and their limitations

Traditional Ni-based cermets, including Ni-YSZ \[\], Ni-SDC \[\], and Ni-ScYSZ \[\], have long dominated SOEC cathode design because of their excellent electrochemical activity for H <sub>2</sub> O and CO <sub>2</sub> reduction. For example, Ni-YSZ electrodes with high Ni loading can achieve CO <sub>2</sub> conversion rates above 80%, with CH <sub>4</sub> selectivity approaching 99% under electrochemically assisted methanation conditions at 300–350 °C \[\]. These results demonstrate that Ni-based electrodes can provide highly active sites for electrochemical reduction and, under suitable conditions, promote methane formation.

However, their application in direct in-situ methanation still faces intrinsic limitations. The surface of Ni-based cermets tends to favor the RWGS reaction, thereby promoting syngas production rather than selective methane formation, even under applied potentials \[\]. Moreover, these electrodes usually require high operating temperatures above 700 °C to maintain sufficient ionic conductivity and electrolysis rates. In addition, Ni-based cermets are susceptible to carbon deposition, Ni coarsening, redox instability, and mechanical degradation in CO <sub>2</sub> -rich or carbon-containing atmospheres, which raises serious concerns regarding long-term durability \[,\]. Although microstructural engineering \[,\] and compositional regulation \[,,, \] have improved the electrochemical stability and durability of Ni-based cermets to some extent, these strategies mainly optimize conventional electrolysis performance and do not fundamentally resolve the temperature mismatch that limits direct methane synthesis.

In this context, mixed ionic-electronic conducting (MIEC) oxides have emerged as promising alternatives because of their improved stability in reducing atmospheres, tunable defect chemistry, and lower susceptibility to typical Ni-related degradation pathways such as carbon deposition and metal coarsening. Perovskite-based MIEC frameworks \[,,,,,,,, \] make use of adjustable oxygen-vacancy chemistry to enhance CO <sub>2</sub> adsorption, activation, and electroreduction kinetics, while enabling more flexible operation in medium-temperature ranges of approximately 600–700 °C.

Within these oxide frameworks, oxygen vacancies play a central role in binding and activating CO <sub>2</sub>, while further performance optimization can be achieved through aliovalent doping \[,, \], in-situ exsolution \[,, \], and high-entropy modification strategies \[,\]. In particular, the incorporation of metallic nanoparticles such as Ni, Fe, and Co through B-site doping \[,, \] or reduction-induced exsolution \[,, \] has proven effective for constructing methanation-active sites on stable oxide backbones. For instance, recent studies on SFM decorated with exsolved Ni-Fe nanoparticles have shown that the perovskite matrix can anchor and stabilize active metallic sites, thereby enhancing hydrogenation kinetics and improving overall electrochemical performance \[,, \].

Despite these advances, single-phase oxides and oxide-metal composite cathodes still generally fall short of delivering the high CH <sub>4</sub> selectivity and long-term stability required for practical SOEC-based in-situ methanation. This indicates that further material design must focus not only on improving electrochemical reduction activity, but also on strengthening the catalytic coupling between electrolysis-derived intermediates and methane-forming pathways.

#### 3.1.2. Multifunctional catalysts: coupling thermal catalysis and electrocatalysis

The most direct response to the thermo-kinetic mismatch is to construct a multifunctional cathode that decouples or compensates for the opposing temperature requirements of electrolysis and methanation. Advances in electrode and electrolyte materials have enabled SOEC operation at progressively lower temperatures, which are often below 700 °C \[,\] and in some cases approaching 600 °C \[\]. And partially alleviate the thermodynamic constraints on methane formation. Under these conditions, the synergistic interaction between electrochemical activation and catalytic hydrogenation becomes increasingly important.

Two principal approaches have been widely explored to realize thermo-electrocatalytic coupling ().

The first involves the introduction of catalytic functional layers on top of conventional SOEC cathodes. In this architecture, the underlying electrode performs electrochemical reduction of CO <sub>2</sub> and H <sub>2</sub> O, generating CO and H <sub>2</sub> intermediates, while the overlying catalyst layer promotes rapid methanation. Functional layers containing catalysts such as CuSn-CGO, Ni-modified LSFCO (La <sub>0.6</sub> Sr <sub>0.4</sub> Fe <sub>0.8</sub> Co <sub>0.2</sub> O <sub>3</sub>), or Ni-Fe/CGO \[,, \] are typically deposited by impregnation, spraying, or screen-printing techniques. Representative studies demonstrate that the introduction of such catalytic coatings can significantly enhance methane selectivity, in some cases increasing CH <sub>4</sub> selectivity from negligible levels to above 90% in the temperature range of 525–600 °C \[\] (). In addition to promoting methanation, the catalytic layer may also protect the Ni-YSZ cermet from reoxidation during operation.

However, the introduction of catalytic coatings can also increase the cell's series resistance and introduce mechanical instability at the cathode-electrolyte interface. Post-operation analyses frequently reveal microstructural fractures in this region, indicating that further optimization of catalyst distribution, porosity, and interfacial compatibility is required (). In addition, long-term catalyst durability remains insufficiently explored \[\].

The second approach involves composite catalytic cathodes, where methanation catalysts are directly incorporated into the electrode structure. To optimize the utilization of medium-temperature SOEC under favorable operating conditions, it is imperative to introduce methanation catalysts that are highly selective and stable, facilitating seamless integration with the SOEC co-electrolysis process \[,,, \]. Among these, transition-metal catalysts supported on oxide matrices, which including Ni, Co, Fe, and Mn species dispersed on CeO <sub>2</sub> -based supports, have shown promising methanation activity under electrochemically assisted conditions. And the Ni-based catalyst achieved a CO conversion of 98.5% at 375 °C, with CH <sub>4</sub> selectivity approaching 80% \[\].

Bimetallic catalysts are especially attractive because their electronic and structural properties can be adjusted to achieve better catalytic performance and higher thermal stability. For example, adding precious metals such as Ru and Pd into Ni-GDC cathodes \[\] can generate a hydrogen spillover effect from the noble metal sites to nearby Ni species, which improves CO methanation activity. Fe and Co are also commonly used because they are inexpensive and abundant \[\]. As a result, Ni-Fe \[,,, \] and Ni-Co alloy \[\] systems have been widely studied for their ability to tune syngas composition and enhance methane selectivity.

Mechanistic insights summarized in Section provide an important basis for the design of thermo-electrocatalytic architectures. These studies show that an applied potential can alter catalyst surface chemistry and thereby enhance methanation activity, even under temperatures that would normally be unfavorable for CH <sub>4</sub> formation. A representative example is the Fe-Ag and Fe-GDC-Ag composite cathode reported by Biswas et al. \[\], in which the increase in methane production was attributed to a synergistic interplay between higher current density (a and b) and potential-induced modification of the Fe work function. The change in the electronic state of Fe is believed to be crucial for promoting hydrogen spillover: atomic hydrogen (H∗) generated by electrocatalytic H <sub>2</sub> O dissociation can rapidly migrate through metallic pathways to neighboring methanation-active sites, thus directly coupling electrolysis with catalytic hydrogenation (c). Notably, although high temperatures generally disfavor methanation from a thermodynamic perspective, the combined effects of applied polarization, enhanced current density, and work-function regulation can shift the kinetic optimum toward higher temperatures, allowing maximum methanation performance to appear at around 600 °C. This case clearly illustrates that thermo-electrocatalytic coupling architectures are not simply physical combinations of electrolysis and catalytic components. Rather, they are material-level realizations of the NEMCA effect and hydrogen-spillover-assisted coupling mechanisms identified from mechanistic studies.

### 3.2. Anode electrodes and electrolytes: matching the requirements of cathode-centered in-situ methanation

Compared with cathode materials, oxygen electrodes and electrolytes play a more indirect but equally important role in SOEC in-situ methanation. Their significance lies not only in improving electrochemical efficiency but also in determining whether the cathode can operate within a temperature range and interfacial environment suitable for the coupling of co-electrolysis and methanation. From this perspective, the design of oxygen electrodes and electrolytes should not be considered separately from cathode development. Instead, they should be evaluated according to how effectively they support cathode-centered medium-temperature operation, reduce ohmic and polarization losses, and maintain stable electrode-electrolyte interfaces under strongly coupled thermo-electrochemical conditions.

#### 3.2.1. Anodes (oxygen electrodes): reducing polarization loss to mitigate the thermo-kinetic mismatch

Although methane is formed at the cathode, the oxygen electrode strongly influences the overall cell efficiency by determining polarization resistance, accessible operating temperature, and long-term stability. In particular, lowering oxygen-electrode polarization losses can reduce the high-temperature requirement typically needed for efficient co-electrolysis, thereby enabling SOEC operation at lower temperatures where methanation is thermodynamically more favorable.

Early oxygen-electrode materials such as Sr-doped lanthanum manganite (LSM) \[,,, \] are not well suited for low-temperature SOEC operation because their predominantly electronic conductivity confines the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) to the three-phase boundary (TPB), resulting in a limited active area, sluggish kinetics, and a need for relatively high operating temperatures due to poor oxygen-ion transport. This further aggravates the mismatch between the optimal conditions for electrolysis and methanation. To address these limitations, mixed ionic-electronic conductors (MIECs), including La <sub>0.6</sub> Sr <sub>0.4</sub> Co <sub>0.2</sub> Fe <sub>0.8</sub> O <sub>3</sub> (LSCF) \[,\], Ba <sub>0.5</sub> Sr <sub>0.5</sub> Co <sub>0.8</sub> Fe <sub>0.2</sub> O <sub>3</sub> (BSCF) \[\], La <sub>2– <em>x</em></sub> Sr <sub><em>x</em></sub> Co <sub>0.5</sub> Ni <sub>0.5</sub> O <sub>4±δ</sub> (LSCN) \[\], and Co- and Sr-free alternatives like Ba <sub>0.95</sub> LaFeO <sub>3-δ</sub> (BLF) \[\], have been widely explored, as their dual ionic/electronic conductivity extends the electrochemically active region beyond the TPB, thereby improving OER kinetics and lowering polarization resistance at medium temperatures (500–650 °C).

Moreover, oxygen electrodes with tailored microstructures \[\], increased porosity, and improved surface oxygen exchange rates further enable efficient oxygen evolution at intermediate temperatures. This reduces the overall cell voltage needed for co-electrolysis and increases the fraction of thermal energy utilized, which is especially impactful in two-temperature-zone reactor designs. For example, the use of Ruddlesden-Popper (RP) oxides \[,,, \] and SFM-based double perovskites \[,\], has been shown to enhance durability and lower polarization resistance while preserving compatibility with cathode-side methanation conditions (). Furthermore, composite electrodes \[,\] and nanostructured electrodes prepared by impregnation, exsolution, or heterostructure construction \[\], integrating complementary properties of multiple phases (e.g., electronic/ionic conductivity), exhibit superior electrical conductivity and electrochemical activity to synergistically support low-temperature cathode functionality.

Additionally, to support the development of proton-conducting electrolytes that are better suited for in-situ methanation, future research on oxygen electrode materials should focus on developing tri-conductive oxides (TCOs) capable of simultaneously conducting oxygen ions, electrons, and protons.

#### 3.2.2. Electrolytes: lowering the effective operating temperature and stabilizing cathode interfaces

An ideal electrolyte for this application should have high ionic conductivity, negligible electronic leakage, sufficient gas tightness, and good chemical compatibility with adjacent electrodes. Just as importantly, it should maintain phase stability and strong interfacial adhesion under the wide oxygen partial pressure gradient encountered during SOEC operation \[,,,,, \]. For in-situ methanation, these requirements become even stricter because the electrolyte must support long-term operation under conditions where cathode materials are exposed to reactive CO <sub>2</sub>, H <sub>2</sub> O, CO, H <sub>2</sub>, CH <sub>4</sub> mixtures and possible carbon-forming environments, which means the electrolyte should not be judged only by ionic conductivity under traditional SOEC conditions, but by whether it can help establish a lower-temperature operating window while maintaining chemical and mechanical stability.

Traditional YSZ-based electrolytes remain widely used because of their excellent chemical and structural stability; however, their relatively low ionic conductivity at intermediate temperatures generally requires SOECs to operate above 700 °C. Although such temperatures are acceptable for co-electrolysis, they are not ideal for methanation. In contrast, medium-temperature electrolytes such as Sc- or Sc/Y-stabilized ZrO <sub>2</sub>, Ln <sub>2</sub> O <sub>3</sub> (Ln = Dy, Er, Y, Gd, Nd, La)-doped ẟ-Bi <sub>2</sub> O <sub>3</sub>, Gd/Sm/Y-doped CeO <sub>2</sub>, and perovskite oxides based on BaCeO <sub>3-ẟ</sub>, BaZrO <sub>3-ẟ</sub>, and BaCe <sub>x</sub> Zr <sub>1-x</sub> O <sub>3-ẟ</sub>. Perovskite oxides, such as La <sub>0.9</sub> Sr <sub>0.1</sub> Ga <sub>0.8</sub> Mg <sub>0.2</sub> O <sub>3</sub> (LSGM) are more attractive for direct methane synthesis because they help lower the cell operating temperature. Among them, LSGM has shown particularly promising performance in methane-producing SOEC systems, with reports of 50% CH <sub>4</sub> yield at 550 °C and 1.3 V, and even higher CO <sub>2</sub> conversion under pressurized counter-current operation \[,,,,,,\]. These results illustrate that electrolyte selection is closely tied to the ability of the cathode to function as both an electrolysis electrode and a methanation-active interface.

Proton-conducting perovskite electrolytes based on BaZrO <sub>3</sub>, BaCeO <sub>3,</sub> and their solid solutions have also received considerable attention because they can enable lower-temperature operation than oxygen-ion conductors \[\]. Their lower activation energy for ionic transport makes them attractive for narrowing the thermo-kinetic mismatch. To improve both conductivity and stability, a large number of doped systems have been developed, including BZCYYb-type materials \[,, \]. Chen et al. \[\] further reported Si-doped BZCYYb, which retained good electrochemical performance while improving mechanical hardness. From the perspective of direct methanation, the importance of these proton-conducting electrolytes lies in their potential to shift the entire cell toward a more methanation-compatible temperature regime. In addition to the development of novel low- and medium-temperature electrolyte materials, nanotechnology, high-entropy modification, and other material enhancement techniques have demonstrated significant potential in enhancing the ionic conductivity and durability of low-temperature SOEC electrolytes. This improvement can be attributed to their unique surface and interfacial properties, as well as the effects associated with nano- and high-entropy materials \[,\].

At the same time, electrolyte compatibility with the cathode cannot be overlooked. Some promising electrolytes, such as LSGM, may react with conventional Ni-based fuel electrodes during fabrication or long-term operation \[\]. This means that a high-conductivity electrolyte is not necessarily an optimal choice unless it can be matched with cathode materials that maintain interfacial stability. Therefore, electrolyte development for in-situ methanation should follow a co-design principle, in which ionic transport properties, sintering behavior, chemical compatibility, and cathode integration are considered together.

Beyond compositional optimization, reducing electrolyte thickness is another effective approach for decreasing ohmic resistance and expanding the medium-temperature operating window. Accordingly, electrode-supported thin-electrolyte configurations have become increasingly important in direct-methanation SOECs.

More broadly, oxygen electrodes and electrolytes should be regarded as supporting components within a cathode-centered in-situ methanation system, rather than as isolated elements optimized independently. Future material development should move beyond the separate optimization of individual cell components and instead focus on the integrated matching of cathode, oxygen electrode, and electrolyte. In the context of direct methane synthesis, the goal is not simply to maximize electrolysis current density, but to establish a stable and efficient operating window in which electrolysis and methanation can proceed synergistically. From this perspective, oxygen electrodes should minimize the kinetic penalties associated with lower-temperature electrolysis, while electrolytes should reduce ohmic losses and maintain stable interfaces with multifunctional cathodes. Only through this kind of system-level materials coordination can the full potential of SOEC-based in-situ methanation be realized.

## 4\. Reactor design for SOEC in-situ methanation: addressing thermo-kinetic mismatch

The mismatch of temperature makes reactor design more than a simple issue of cell geometry. Its main role is to create a reaction environment in which electrolysis and methanation can be coupled as effectively as possible \[,\]. From this viewpoint, current reactor designs can be discussed according to how they deal with the temperature mismatch and organize the reaction field.

### 4.1. Configuration comparison: single-temperature-zone vs. two-temperature-zone reactors

#### 4.1.1. Single-temperature-zone reactors

Single-temperature-zone systems are the simplest approach to reactor design, where the cathode acts as both the electrolysis electrode and the methanation-active surface, or is directly coupled with an additional catalyst layer. Planar SOECs are the most common implementation (a), offering structural simplicity and high power-density. Fuel-electrode-supported cells reduce ohmic losses through thinner electrolytes, making lower-temperature operation feasible and slightly favoring methanation. Metal-supported cells may provide additional benefits in heat management, although their long-term durability still requires further validation \[\]. However, methane formation remains thermodynamically and kinetically limited under uniform high-temperature conditions, as demonstrated by low yields from electrolyte-supported (∼0.2% CH <sub>4</sub> \[\]) and cathode-supported (∼0.286% CH <sub>4</sub> \[\]) SOECs. Although increased pressure and gas flow optimization can promote methane formation, single-zone designs fundamentally cannot resolve the intrinsic temperature mismatch between electrolysis and methanation, and their practical performance continues to rely heavily on improvements in cathode materials and catalyst design which discussed in section.

#### 4.1.2. Two-temperature-zone reactors

In two-temperature-zone configurations, electrolysis and methanation are spatially separated so that each process can proceed under its respective optimal conditions. Tubular and microtubular reactors are particularly well suited to this strategy (b), as their axial geometry naturally enables temperature zoning. For example, Chen et al. \[\] reported a CH <sub>4</sub> yield of 11.84% in a tubular reactor, while Lei et al. \[\] further increased the yield to 21.1% using a microtubular design. Additional improvements in CO <sub>2</sub> conversion and methane yield can be achieved through pressurization \[\] and optimization of the relative lengths of the two temperature zones \[\].

Despite their advantages, dual-zone reactors pose considerable engineering challenges, especially in elongated tubular systems, where axial temperature gradients can generate substantial thermomechanical stress and thus compromise structural integrity \[\]. Therefore, maintaining stable temperature gradients and alleviating thermal stress are key considerations in the design of two-temperature-zone SOEC reactors.

Flow-field optimization has emerged as an effective strategy to address these issues. Reported studies indicate that rotating L-shaped flow fields can achieve the most uniform gas distribution and the highest hydrogen production efficiency, although thermal stress may become concentrated at rib edges. In contrast, parallel flow fields exhibit the lowest thermal stress and are therefore more suitable for durability-oriented designs \[\]. Rectangular flow channels have also been shown to improve fuel utilization compared with square and trapezoidal geometries, albeit at the expense of less uniform stress distribution \[\]. Meanwhile, cross-flow configurations provide a promising balance between electrochemical performance and mechanical stability by simultaneously enhancing hydrogen production efficiency and reducing thermal stress \[\]. Counter-flow arrangements further improve temperature uniformity, while the use of metal foam as a flow distributor can lower both the average temperature and thermal stress, although this may involve trade-offs in pressure drop and fabrication complexity \[\]. Beyond flow-field design, additional thermal-management strategies have also proven effective in improving temperature uniformity and relieving stress throughout reactor components. These include external air cooling \[\], the integration of planar high-temperature heat pipes \[\], regulation of inlet gas temperature, and dynamic adjustment of anode gas flow \[\]. Collectively, these approaches not only enhance SOEC performance and durability, but also provide important guidance for the design and long-term stable operation of dual-zone tubular reactors.

To combine the respective advantages of planar and tubular architectures, flat-tubular SOECs have also been proposed \[\] (c). These systems offer a potential compromise between compactness and mechanical robustness. However, research on flat-tubular SOEC reactors remains relatively limited. Further experimental and modeling studies are still needed to optimize operating conditions, understand thermal matching among materials, and establish the theoretical basis required for structural design and practical engineering implementation of flat-tubular SOECs \[\]. ### 4.2. Flow-field designs: the potential to mitigate the temperature mismatch

The role of flow-field engineering in thermal management extends beyond the mitigation of thermal stress, as it also offers an effective route to alleviate thermo-kinetic mismatch. Advanced flow-channel geometries, including trapezoidal \[\], elliptical \[\], and X-type designs \[\], can improve localized heat transfer by modifying gas-flow behavior and enhancing interactions between reactants and reactive surfaces. In doing so, these configurations promote more uniform heat and mass transfer, reduce local “dead zones,” and facilitate more consistent oxygen transport to active reaction sites. For example, staggered or discrete rib structures \[\] enable multidirectional gas diffusion while reducing oxygen concentration gradients beneath the ribs, thereby shortening diffusion pathways and accelerating reactant replenishment. These effects are particularly relevant to SOECs, where improved transport and thermal uniformity can help relieve local temperature–reaction mismatches.

More sophisticated geometries, such as trapezoidal flow channels \[\], offer additional advantages by extending gas residence time. In SOEC systems targeting methane production, longer residence times for CO <sub>2</sub>, H <sub>2</sub>, and CO increase contact between gaseous reactants and catalytic sites, thereby promoting the hydrogenation of CO to methane. At the same time, the gradual tapering of ribs and channels helps optimize flow distribution, suppress hot-spot formation, and maintain more uniform heat transfer along the channel. Such features are especially important in two-temperature-zone SOECs, where precise regulation of reactant transport and temperature is required to balance the distinct demands of electrolysis and methanation.

Although most of these advances in flow-field design have been developed in the context of SOFCs, the underlying principles are highly transferable to SOEC systems. Therefore, adapting optimized flow fields and current-path designs from SOFC research holds considerable promise for improving SOEC operational stability and methane-production efficiency. In this sense, flow-field engineering provides not only a practical foundation for enhancing in-situ methane synthesis, but also a broader design framework for SOEC applications requiring precise thermal and kinetic control.

In addition, appropriate catalyst placement within the flow field is critical for thermally intensified reactor designs. Direct catalyst coating on the cathode surface (a) can enhance methane selectivity, with reported CH <sub>4</sub> selectivity reaching 44%, but this strategy also increases sensitivity to temperature fluctuations. By contrast, combining cathode-supported catalyst coating with a downstream fixed-bed catalyst \[\] can significantly improve both CO <sub>2</sub> conversion (∼70.5%) and CH <sub>4</sub> selectivity (∼99.6%) by extending gas residence time and redistributing the heat released during methanation (b and c).

Overall, single-temperature-zone reactors are simple, but their methane yield is limited by the narrow overlap between electrolysis and methanation conditions. Two-temperature-zone reactors provide a more direct solution by separating the two functions in space, although they also introduce greater thermal and mechanical complexity. Flow-engineered and thermally intensified reactors further improve local matching by optimizing heat transfer, residence time, and catalyst utilization. To mitigate the temperature mismatch, high-performance reactor design for SOEC in-situ methanation should be understood as a combined problem of geometry, support configuration, catalyst placement, pressure, and flow arrangement \[,,,,,\], rather than a simple choice of reactor shape.

## 5\. Conclusion and outlook

The development of SOEC-based in-situ methanation is fundamentally limited by the thermo-kinetic mismatch between high-temperature co-electrolysis and low-temperature methanation. This mismatch represents the core scientific and engineering challenge of the field and also explains why meaningful progress cannot rely on the isolated optimization of any single component, but instead requires the coordinated advancement of reaction mechanisms, functional materials, and reactor configurations.

From a mechanistic standpoint, methane formation in SOECs results from the coupling of several pathways, including direct electrochemical CO <sub>2</sub> reduction, the RWGS reaction, thermocatalytic hydrogenation, and carbon-related side reactions. Nevertheless, the relative contributions of these pathways under realistic operating conditions remain insufficiently understood. Although electrochemical promotion effects, such as NEMCA and hydrogen spillover, are increasingly considered to be potentially important in facilitating methane formation, their quantitative roles under practical SOEC conditions have not yet been clearly established.

At the materials level, current research is moving beyond conventional electrolysis-oriented cathodes toward multifunctional cathode architectures that combine co-electrolysis activity with methanation capability. Oxygen electrodes and electrolytes should be selected and designed according to how effectively they support cathode-centered operation in the medium-temperature range, reduce polarization and ohmic losses, and maintain stable interfaces with methanation-active cathodes. The goal of materials design is not simply to maximize electrolysis current density, but to establish a stable and efficient operating window in which electrolysis and methanation can proceed in a coordinated manner.

At the reactor level, current research clearly shows that reactor engineering is an important means of mitigating the thermo-kinetic mismatch. Single-temperature-zone reactors are structurally simple, but their methane productivity is constrained by the limited overlap between electrolysis and methanation conditions. Two-temperature-zone reactors offer a more direct solution by spatially separating the high-temperature electrolysis region from the lower-temperature methanation region, while thermally intensified and flow-engineered designs further improve local compatibility through control of heat transfer, gas residence time, catalyst distribution, and pressure. These studies indicate that efficient methane production depends not only on reactor geometry, but also on reactor-operation coupling, including the coordinated optimization of temperature, pressure, flow mode, and catalyst placement.

At the system level, one of the principal advantages of in-situ methanation lies in its intra-system heat utilization, namely, the direct feedback of methanation heat to support the endothermic electrolysis process \[\]. In this review, the temperature mismatch issue associated with SOEC-based in-situ methane production has been systematically analyzed, and feasible strategies for temperature zoning have been discussed in detail. Beyond reactor-level thermal matching, however, efficient heat integration at the system level is equally critical to the engineering viability of the overall SOEC-to-methane process. A first key approach is heat-exchange design. By integrating heat exchangers between the methanation and electrolysis zones, the exothermic heat released during methanation can be transferred to preheat the electrolysis feed, thereby reducing the external energy input. A representative example is the design in which a copper tube was inserted at the SOEC outlet to introduce low-temperature CO <sub>2</sub>, allowing it to mix with the high-temperature cathode off-gas and thus realize direct thermal recovery \[\]. A second important strategy is the incorporation of thermal storage units at critical locations, such as thermal energy storage (TES) \[\] modules or liquid CO <sub>2</sub> energy storage tanks (LCES) \[\]. These components can buffer transient thermal loads, suppress temperature fluctuations, and improve operational stability under dynamic conditions. At a broader process scale, coupling the SOEC-methanation system with thermodynamic power cycles offers an effective route for valorizing surplus heat \[\]. For example, integration with an organic Rankine cycle (ORC) has been shown to enable substantial heat recovery in O-SOEC-based CO <sub>2</sub> -to-CH <sub>4</sub> systems, leading to a methane production efficiency of 86% and an overall process efficiency of 77% \[\]. In another case, combining an ORC with a double-effect Li/Br absorption refrigeration cycle (ARC) in a geothermal-power-driven CO <sub>2</sub> -to-CH <sub>4</sub> system reduced the energy demand for methane production by 35% and shortened the payback period by 19%. Notably, the use of isopropanol as the ORC working fluid further reduced the payback period to 7.419 years \[\].

Additionally, Long-term stability is still one of the core bottlenecks in the engineering application of SOEC in-situ methanation. Compared with conventional SOEC, the SOEC-methanation coupling system simultaneously withstands high-temperature electrolysis, strong reducing atmosphere, exothermic methanation reaction and complex atmosphere composition changes, and its degradation mechanism is more diverse and coupled. Firstly, the Ni-based fuel electrode/catalyst phase is prone to particle coarsening, migration and redistribution under high temperature, high water vapor partial pressure or strong local temperature gradient, resulting in a decrease in the length of the three-phase interface, a decrease in electrochemical activity, and a possible weakening of methanation catalytic activity. Secondly, due to the significant thermodynamic and kinetic differences between the electrolysis zone and the methanation zone, the local thermal expansion mismatch may induce electrode-electrolyte interface peeling, contact resistance increase and mechanical crack propagation, especially in the process of start-stop, load fluctuation and temperature zone switching. In addition, under the co-existence atmosphere of CO <sub>2</sub> /CO/H <sub>2</sub>, side reactions such as Boudouard reaction, CO disproportionation and methane cracking may lead to carbon deposition, blocking channels, covering active sites and accelerating the destruction of electrode structure. The above process not only reduces the electrolysis performance and methane generation efficiency, but also further enlarges the local temperature and concentration gradient, forming a positive feedback instability of ‘heat-mass transfer-structure degradation’.

Despite these promising advances, the commercialization of direct methane synthesis via SOEC co-electrolysis still faces substantial challenges. Future research should therefore focus on progress in the following areas:
- (1)
	In-Depth Development of Catalysts and Cell Materials: Future efforts should focus on designing cathode, anode, and electrolyte materials that operate efficiently within the medium-temperature range (∼550 °C) with high ionic-electronic conductivity, superior methanation activity, and strong resistance to carbon deposition, and enhancing the compatibility between electrolytes and catalytic cathodes under low-temperature operating conditions. Greater emphasis should be placed on understanding and exploiting the synergistic interactions between bimetallic or multimetallic alloy nanocatalysts and cathode substrates, including cermet and perovskite-based frameworks. Approaches such as high-entropy engineering \[\] and elemental doping offer effective means to regulate the intrinsic electronic structure of materials as well as their oxygen-vacancy concentration. In parallel, techniques such as infiltration and in situ exsolution should be employed to construct nanostructured active sites with extended triple-phase boundaries and strengthened metal-support interactions. Furthermore, the integration of artificial intelligence with high-throughput computational methods is expected to accelerate the discovery, screening, and performance prediction of emerging material systems, including high-entropy oxides and ternary mixed ionic-electronic conductors.
- (2)
	In-situ Resolution of Reaction Mechanisms and Interfacial Processes: The quantitative contributions of individual reaction pathways remain insufficiently clarified, particularly with respect to the NEMCA effect and its distinction from other polarization-induced phenomena, such as local H <sub>2</sub> enrichment, oxygen-vacancy formation, work-function modulation, and interfacial charge transfer. At the same time, there is an urgent need to develop and apply advanced in-situ and operando characterization techniques, including APXPS and operando Raman/SEM, to track in real time the evolution of active sites, the formation of reaction intermediates, and the pathways of ionic and electronic transport at the triple-phase boundary under realistic operating conditions. Such efforts will provide direct mechanistic insight and a stronger theoretical basis for material design and operating-parameter optimization.
- (3)
	System Integration and Long-Term Stability Enhancement: Future research should place greater emphasis on clarifying the fundamental causes of degradation, including nickel migration and agglomeration, electrode poisoning, and sealant failure. An integrated strategy that combines theoretical simulation with in-situ and operando characterization is essential for revealing both reaction and degradation mechanisms at the atomic scale. Density functional theory can be used to predict reaction pathways and associated energy barriers, while techniques such as in-situ X-ray diffraction, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, and environmental transmission electron microscopy provide real-time insight into the structural and chemical evolution of electrode materials. Beyond reporting short-term electrochemical performance and methane yield, future studies should also establish a systematic framework for long-term durability assessment relevant to engineering applications, including constant-current and dynamic-load testing, thermal cycling, start-stop cycling, carbon-deposition tolerance evaluation, and post-failure multiscale structural characterization. The combination of degradation mechanism analysis and reactor design optimization can provide more reliable design basis for life prediction, material screening and engineering amplification of SOEC in-situ methanation system.

Overall, SOEC in-situ methanation is evolving from proof-of-concept studies toward a more integrated scientific and engineering framework. At present, two bottlenecks appear to be particularly decisive for future progress. One is the absence of a quantitative mechanistic description of NEMCA-type electrochemical promotion under realistic methanation conditions, which limits the rational design of thermo-electrocatalytic cathodes. The other is the insufficient compatibility of existing electrolyte/electrode systems with the medium-to low-temperature regime required for selective methane formation, where ionic transport, interfacial stability, and long-term durability become increasingly critical. Future research should therefore prioritize, first, operando and modeling-based quantification of electric-field effects, surface oxygen coverage, and competitive methane-formation pathways; and second, the development of cathode-centered integrated materials systems that can sustain efficient co-electrolysis and methanation under medium-temperature conditions. Combined with reactor designs that enable precise thermal and flow-field regulation, these advances will be essential for translating SOEC-based direct methane synthesis into a practical power-to-methane technology.

In-Depth Development of Catalysts and Cell Materials: Future efforts should focus on designing cathode, anode, and electrolyte materials that operate efficiently within the medium-temperature range (∼550 °C) with high ionic-electronic conductivity, superior methanation activity, and strong resistance to carbon deposition, and enhancing the compatibility between electrolytes and catalytic cathodes under low-temperature operating conditions. Greater emphasis should be placed on understanding and exploiting the synergistic interactions between bimetallic or multimetallic alloy nanocatalysts and cathode substrates, including cermet and perovskite-based frameworks. Approaches such as high-entropy engineering \[\] and elemental doping offer effective means to regulate the intrinsic electronic structure of materials as well as their oxygen-vacancy concentration. In parallel, techniques such as infiltration and in situ exsolution should be employed to construct nanostructured active sites with extended triple-phase boundaries and strengthened metal-support interactions. Furthermore, the integration of artificial intelligence with high-throughput computational methods is expected to accelerate the discovery, screening, and performance prediction of emerging material systems, including high-entropy oxides and ternary mixed ionic-electronic conductors.

In-situ Resolution of Reaction Mechanisms and Interfacial Processes: The quantitative contributions of individual reaction pathways remain insufficiently clarified, particularly with respect to the NEMCA effect and its distinction from other polarization-induced phenomena, such as local H <sub>2</sub> enrichment, oxygen-vacancy formation, work-function modulation, and interfacial charge transfer. At the same time, there is an urgent need to develop and apply advanced in-situ and operando characterization techniques, including APXPS and operando Raman/SEM, to track in real time the evolution of active sites, the formation of reaction intermediates, and the pathways of ionic and electronic transport at the triple-phase boundary under realistic operating conditions. Such efforts will provide direct mechanistic insight and a stronger theoretical basis for material design and operating-parameter optimization.

System Integration and Long-Term Stability Enhancement: Future research should place greater emphasis on clarifying the fundamental causes of degradation, including nickel migration and agglomeration, electrode poisoning, and sealant failure. An integrated strategy that combines theoretical simulation with in-situ and operando characterization is essential for revealing both reaction and degradation mechanisms at the atomic scale. Density functional theory can be used to predict reaction pathways and associated energy barriers, while techniques such as in-situ X-ray diffraction, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, and environmental transmission electron microscopy provide real-time insight into the structural and chemical evolution of electrode materials. Beyond reporting short-term electrochemical performance and methane yield, future studies should also establish a systematic framework for long-term durability assessment relevant to engineering applications, including constant-current and dynamic-load testing, thermal cycling, start-stop cycling, carbon-deposition tolerance evaluation, and post-failure multiscale structural characterization. The combination of degradation mechanism analysis and reactor design optimization can provide more reliable design basis for life prediction, material screening and engineering amplification of SOEC in-situ methanation system.
