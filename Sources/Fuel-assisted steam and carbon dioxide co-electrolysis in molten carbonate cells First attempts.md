## 1\. Introduction

The mitigation of anthropogenic CO <sub>2</sub> emissions is one of the major challenges associated with the ongoing energy transition, making the development of effective Carbon Capture, Utilization, and Storage (CCUS) strategies a critical priority. Beyond conventional sequestration approaches,, the integration of CO <sub>2</sub> capture with conversion into value-added products represents a particularly attractive pathway to reduce the carbon footprint of energy systems,. In this context, hydrogen production technologies enabling the simultaneous utilization of CO <sub>2</sub> have gained increasing attention,. High-temperature co-electrolysis of H <sub>2</sub> O and CO <sub>2</sub> provides a direct route to convert electrical energy into synthesis gas, a mixture of H <sub>2</sub> and CO, which can be subsequently upgraded into synthetic fuels and chemicals,.

Among high-temperature electrolysis technologies, Solid Oxide Electrolysis Cells (SOECs), operating typically between 700 and 1000 °C, have been extensively investigated for both steam electrolysis and the co-electrolysis of H <sub>2</sub> O and CO <sub>2</sub>,. In co-electrolysis mode, SOECs enable the direct production of syngas, which can be subsequently converted into liquid fuels and chemicals through well-established processes such as Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

In parallel, Molten Carbonate Electrolysis Cells (MCECs) have recently attracted growing attention as an alternative high-temperature electrolysis technology capable of simultaneously producing hydrogen and capturing CO <sub>2</sub> at temperatures between 550 and 700 °C,. Compared to SOECs, which have undergone more extensive development and industrial implementation, research on MCECs is still at an early stage. Recent studies have addressed, among others, the co-electrolysis of H <sub>2</sub> O and CO <sub>2</sub> for synthetic gas generation, advanced electrochemical diagnostics of the fuel electrode, numerical modeling aimed at performance optimization and carbon deposition mitigation, reversible operation,, and dynamic behavior at increasing system scale. Water electrolysis is thermodynamically favored at elevated temperatures (typically above 600 °C), where the chemical potential required to split water into hydrogen and oxygen is reduced. In addition, high operating temperatures enhance reaction kinetics, lowering electrode polarization losses and improving overall system efficiency. Nevertheless, electricity remains the dominant contributor to the cost of hydrogen production by electrolysis, accounting for approximately 50–70% of the total cost. This limitation is closely related to the intrinsic operating principle of high-temperature electrolysis cells, which behave as oxygen concentration cells. The reducing atmosphere at the cathode and the oxygen-rich environment typically present at the anode generate a large oxygen partial pressure gradient across the electrolyte, resulting in a high open-circuit voltage (OCV), on the order of ∼1 V at around 800 °C. The OCV, as an experimentally measurable quantity related to the equilibrium cell potential, represents the thermodynamic baseline of the system under given operating conditions. The actual electrical energy demand is determined by the operating voltage required to sustain a given current density, which includes the contribution of the equilibrium potential and additional overpotentials associated with electrode kinetics, mass transport, and ohmic losses. In water electrolysis, a significant fraction of the applied voltage is typically associated with the oxygen evolution reaction at the anode, which contributes significantly to the overall energy demand due to its high overpotential,. In this context, fuel-assisted operation aims to replace or complement this reaction with alternative anodic pathways, thereby reducing both the equilibrium potential and the associated overpotentials. This operating concept is commonly referred to as fuel-assisted (FA) electrolysis and, so far, it has been studied only in SOECs. When H <sub>2</sub> or CO are employed as assisting fuels, the amount of fuel consumed at the anode is stoichiometrically equivalent to the amount of H <sub>2</sub> and CO produced at the cathode during H <sub>2</sub> O/CO <sub>2</sub> electrolysis, making this approach unattractive from a practical and economic standpoint. Nevertheless, it may become viable in specific process scenarios where low-value or diluted hydrogen-containing streams are available, enabling their effective utilization within the system. In this context, H <sub>2</sub> - or CO- assisted operation is still valuable from an experimental perspective, as it allows the identification of a key operating parameter known as the turning point current density (). This occurs when the supplied fuel is just enough to react with all the oxygen that would have been produced in conventional electrolysis. At this point, the cell transitions from FA to standard electrolysis: any further increase in current density leads to oxygen evolution at the anode. The exact location of this turning point depends on factors such as gas-phase diffusion and the kinetics of fuel and oxygen reactions. Identifying is essential to characterize FA operation and to quantify the maximum benefit achievable with a given fuel flow rate.

In contrast, the use of hydrocarbons, such as methane, is more interesting as an assisting fuel due to their higher availability. The introduction of fuel to the anode of SOECs was pioneered by Pham et al., who demonstrated that methane as a reducing gas could reduce the operating potential by nearly 50%. Subsequent experiments showed even larger reductions in operating potential, with decreases reported to reach up to one order of magnitude,. These studies also highlighted the capability of FASOECs to perform co-electrolysis of H <sub>2</sub> O and CO <sub>2</sub>, enabling the simultaneous production of hydrogen and carbon monoxide (syngas), which underscores the versatility of this approach,,.

While fuel-assisted SOECs have been widely studied, their implementation in MCECs presents additional challenges, mainly related to different electrolyte chemistry and electrode reaction pathways. To the best of the authors' knowledge, this is the first study addressing fuel-assisted operation in MCECs. In this context, the present work aims not only to assess the feasibility of this operating mode, but also to provide insight into the underlying electrochemical behavior and reaction mechanisms. In these systems, the molten carbonate electrolyte not only requires a minimum CO <sub>2</sub> concentration in the atmosphere to maintain its stability,, but the carbonate ions decompose at the anode to produce oxide ions, releasing CO <sub>2</sub> in the process:(6)

Unlike SOECs, CO <sub>2</sub> in MCECs is generated internally regardless of the anodic inlet gas composition, introducing an additional level of complexity in the implementation of fuel-assisted strategies. The objective of this work is to investigate whether MCECs can operate in FA mode, to identify the associated challenges, and to elucidate the reactions involved. To achieve this, the study was structured into two experimental campaigns: the first conducted at the KTH Royal Institute of Technology in Stockholm, focusing on button cells to assess the feasibility of FA operation, and the second carried out at ENEA research center in Rome, where a larger-scale setup was used for a more in-depth investigation.

After testing the cell under standard conditions (55/30/15 mol% N <sub>2</sub> /CO <sub>2</sub> /O <sub>2</sub>), a stepwise approach was adopted to evaluate the impact of each component on electrochemical performance. The cell was first supplied with pure nitrogen (100 mol% N <sub>2</sub>). Then, to evaluate the feasibility of fuel-assisted electrolysis in MCECs, H <sub>2</sub> was employed as the anodic fuel by supplying a 90/10 mol% N <sub>2</sub> /H <sub>2</sub> mixture. This test aims to confirm the feasibility of the process, although it is not practical from a real-world perspective, since using pure hydrogen as a fuel in FA electrolysis would be of limited relevance.

Furthermore, as discussed in, a CO <sub>2</sub> atmosphere must be maintained at both electrodes to preserve electrolyte stability. Although CO <sub>2</sub> is formed at the anode through carbonate ion decomposition (see Eq. ), the amount produced is still limited because of the low utilization factors obtained in the button cell. In the preliminary N <sub>2</sub> /H <sub>2</sub> test, CO <sub>2</sub> was not supplied intentionally, as this condition was meant to serve only as a proof-of-concept for fuel-assisted electrolysis rather than as a chemically representative operating environment. Therefore, in the subsequent step, a N <sub>2</sub> /CO <sub>2</sub> mixture (95/5 mol%) was fed to the anode, followed by the addition of methane to obtain a 90/5/5 mol% N <sub>2</sub> /CO <sub>2</sub> /CH <sub>4</sub> mixture, in order to assess the potential of using CH <sub>4</sub>, which is less valuable than hydrogen, as a fuel. As reported in, the turning-point current densities calculated for both fuel-assisted electrolysis tests—using either methane or hydrogen—are significantly higher than the current densities typically achievable with button-type MCECs. These values were estimated based on Faraday's law, by calculating the electrochemically generated O <sup>2−</sup> ion flux required to stoichiometrically oxidize the entire fuel feed (H <sub>2</sub> or CH <sub>4</sub>) at the anode. Under such conditions, all O <sup>2−</sup> ions generated by the electrolysis reaction would, in principle, react completely with the fuel at the anode, preventing any excess oxygen from being produced.

Aside from the OCV values, the curves at 100% N <sub>2</sub> and 95/5% N <sub>2</sub> /CO <sub>2</sub> exhibit similar trends. Under these conditions, the reaction taking place at the anode is the standard oxygen evolution from the oxidation of the carbonate ions. The voltage increases almost linearly from OCV to about 1 V, likely because of a pronounced concentration overpotential arising from the limited CO <sub>2</sub> and O <sub>2</sub> availability. As the current density increases and the potential approaches 1 V, the slope of the curves decreases significantly, indicating that CO <sub>2</sub> and O <sub>2</sub> are generated in sufficient amounts to stabilize mass transfer limitations and mitigate the concentration overpotential. Notably, at high current densities, the polarization curves under these two oxygen-deficient anodic compositions exhibit voltage behavior comparable to that obtained under standard conditions.

When methane is introduced at the anode, the OCV remains very low, around 0.1 V, and the resulting polarization curve exhibits two distinct regions characterized by different slopes, indicating a transition between two operating regimes associated with different dominant anodic reactions. At low current densities, up to approximately 60 mA/cm <sup>2</sup>, the electrochemical oxidation of methane appears to prevail, leading to reduced cell voltages. In the intermediate range between 60 and 100 mA/cm <sup>2</sup> a gradual transition between methane-assisted operation and conventional electrolysis is likely to occur. As the current density further increases beyond 100 mA/cm <sup>2</sup> oxygen evolution becomes dominant, and the cell behavior progressively resembles that of standard MCEC operation. This behavior suggests a decrease in methane conversion with increasing current density. From a purely thermodynamic and stoichiometric standpoint, the methane flow rate supplied to the anode (10 Nml/min) would theoretically allow fuel-assisted operation up to current densities of about 450 mA/cm <sup>2</sup>. However, this condition is not observed experimentally. This discrepancy indicates that, although methane electrochemical oxidation occurs at low current densities, its kinetics become increasingly unfavorable compared to oxygen evolution at higher current densities. In addition, at high current densities methane may not have sufficient time to effectively interact with the electrochemically generated oxygen, suggesting the onset of mass-transport and fluid-dynamic limitations. This effect is likely enhanced by the very low gas residence times of the present setup. Conversely, this behavior is not observed when H <sub>2</sub> is used as the anodic fuel, since hydrogen, being significantly more reactive than methane, can be fully oxidized over the investigated current density range.

Overall, the polarization behavior observed in the button cell highlights a complex interplay between fuel oxidation and conventional oxygen evolution under FA electrolysis operation. While electrochemical performance can provide indications of a transition between operating regimes, it does not allow a direct identification of the anodic reactions occurring under different current densities. For this reason, the analysis was extended to the single-cell configuration, which is closer to full-scale cells, where the outlet gas composition was systematically monitored. The results obtained for the single cell are presented and discussed in the following paragraphs.

a shows the polarization curves measured under conventional electrolysis conditions and during FA operation with H <sub>2</sub> supplied as anodic fuel. At this current density, stoichiometric balance is established between the hydrogen produced at the cathode and the hydrogen consumed at the anode. The occurrence of this transition is further confirmed by the anodic gas analysis on a dry basis, reported in b. At low current densities, carbon dioxide is the only gaseous reaction product, as hydrogen reacts with oxide ions to form water. As the current density increases, the CO <sub>2</sub> concentration rises and oxygen production is observed once the turning-point current density is exceeded and the anodic fuel is completely consumed. The results obtained with hydrogen as anodic fuel primarily serve to validate the fuel-assisted electrolysis mechanism in MCECs and to confirm the consistency of the turning-point behavior. ## 4\. Conclusion

A key challenge for the broader development of high-temperature electrolysis technologies is the reduction of electrical energy consumption, which strongly impacts the overall efficiency and economic viability of hydrogen production. This study aims to explore fuel-assisted operation in MCECs, a strategy pursued to reduce the operating voltage and improve the overall energy efficiency of high-temperature electrolysis. The investigation focuses on evaluating the performance of MCECs when hydrogen or methane is supplied as the anodic fuel. The experimental campaign was structured in two stages: first, tests were performed on a small-scale button cell to assess the fundamental feasibility and electrochemical behavior; subsequently, a larger planar cell was employed to validate the findings and allow detailed gas analysis at the anode outlet.

Results from the button cell indicate that at low current densities, methane can actively participate in anodic reactions, contributing to a measurable decrease in cell voltage. As the current density exceeds 100 mA/cm <sup>2</sup>, however, oxygen evolution increasingly dominates, and the cell's behavior approaches that of conventional MCEC operation. This result suggests a lower reactivity of methane toward oxygen ions (O <sup>2−</sup>), particularly at higher current densities.

Tests with the planar cell confirmed that hydrogen can effectively enable fuel-assisted operation, validating the mechanism and reproducing the characteristic turning-point behavior. When methane was used, polarization curves alone were insufficient to directly quantify its conversion. Gas analysis at 700 °C revealed that oxygen evolution dominates at higher currents, while methane oxidation is limited and occurs predominantly at low current densities. Variations in inlet water content had negligible impact on the cell voltage and outlet anodic composition, indicating that under the tested conditions methane decomposition remains kinetically restricted.

Overall, these results demonstrate that fuel-assisted operation in MCECs is feasible with highly reactive fuels such as hydrogen, whereas methane utilization is strongly limited by its low reactivity under the investigated conditions. These findings provide a first mechanistic understanding of FA operation in MCECs and highlight that future improvements should focus on enhancing methane activation through the development of more active anodic materials and/or catalytic phases, as well as optimizing reactor design parameters such as reactant residence time to promote fuel conversion and enable effective operation with less reactive fuels.

The mitigation of anthropogenic CO <sub>2</sub> emissions is one of the major challenges associated with the ongoing energy transition, making the development of effective Carbon Capture, Utilization, and Storage (CCUS) strategies a critical priority. Beyond conventional sequestration approaches,, the integration of CO <sub>2</sub> capture with conversion into value-added products represents a particularly attractive pathway to reduce the carbon footprint of energy systems,. In this context, hydrogen production technologies enabling the simultaneous utilization of CO <sub>2</sub> have gained increasing attention,. High-temperature co-electrolysis of H <sub>2</sub> O and CO <sub>2</sub> provides a direct route to convert electrical energy into synthesis gas, a mixture of H <sub>2</sub> and CO, which can be subsequently upgraded into synthetic fuels and chemicals,.

Among high-temperature electrolysis technologies, Solid Oxide Electrolysis Cells (SOECs), operating typically between 700 and 1000 °C, have been extensively investigated for both steam electrolysis and the co-electrolysis of H <sub>2</sub> O and CO <sub>2</sub>,. In co-electrolysis mode, SOECs enable the direct production of syngas, which can be subsequently converted into liquid fuels and chemicals through well-established processes such as Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

In parallel, Molten Carbonate Electrolysis Cells (MCECs) have recently attracted growing attention as an alternative high-temperature electrolysis technology capable of simultaneously producing hydrogen and capturing CO <sub>2</sub> at temperatures between 550 and 700 °C,. Compared to SOECs, which have undergone more extensive development and industrial implementation, research on MCECs is still at an early stage. Recent studies have addressed, among others, the co-electrolysis of H <sub>2</sub> O and CO <sub>2</sub> for synthetic gas generation, advanced electrochemical diagnostics of the fuel electrode, numerical modeling aimed at performance optimization and carbon deposition mitigation, reversible operation,, and dynamic behavior at increasing system scale. Water electrolysis is thermodynamically favored at elevated temperatures (typically above 600 °C), where the chemical potential required to split water into hydrogen and oxygen is reduced. In addition, high operating temperatures enhance reaction kinetics, lowering electrode polarization losses and improving overall system efficiency. Nevertheless, electricity remains the dominant contributor to the cost of hydrogen production by electrolysis, accounting for approximately 50–70% of the total cost. This limitation is closely related to the intrinsic operating principle of high-temperature electrolysis cells, which behave as oxygen concentration cells. The reducing atmosphere at the cathode and the oxygen-rich environment typically present at the anode generate a large oxygen partial pressure gradient across the electrolyte, resulting in a high open-circuit voltage (OCV), on the order of ∼1 V at around 800 °C. The OCV, as an experimentally measurable quantity related to the equilibrium cell potential, represents the thermodynamic baseline of the system under given operating conditions. The actual electrical energy demand is determined by the operating voltage required to sustain a given current density, which includes the contribution of the equilibrium potential and additional overpotentials associated with electrode kinetics, mass transport, and ohmic losses. In water electrolysis, a significant fraction of the applied voltage is typically associated with the oxygen evolution reaction at the anode, which contributes significantly to the overall energy demand due to its high overpotential,. In this context, fuel-assisted operation aims to replace or complement this reaction with alternative anodic pathways, thereby reducing both the equilibrium potential and the associated overpotentials. This operating concept is commonly referred to as fuel-assisted (FA) electrolysis and, so far, it has been studied only in SOECs. When H <sub>2</sub> or CO are employed as assisting fuels, the amount of fuel consumed at the anode is stoichiometrically equivalent to the amount of H <sub>2</sub> and CO produced at the cathode during H <sub>2</sub> O/CO <sub>2</sub> electrolysis, making this approach unattractive from a practical and economic standpoint. Nevertheless, it may become viable in specific process scenarios where low-value or diluted hydrogen-containing streams are available, enabling their effective utilization within the system. In this context, H <sub>2</sub> - or CO- assisted operation is still valuable from an experimental perspective, as it allows the identification of a key operating parameter known as the turning point current density (). This occurs when the supplied fuel is just enough to react with all the oxygen that would have been produced in conventional electrolysis. At this point, the cell transitions from FA to standard electrolysis: any further increase in current density leads to oxygen evolution at the anode. The exact location of this turning point depends on factors such as gas-phase diffusion and the kinetics of fuel and oxygen reactions. Identifying is essential to characterize FA operation and to quantify the maximum benefit achievable with a given fuel flow rate.

While fuel-assisted SOECs have been widely studied, their implementation in MCECs presents additional challenges, mainly related to different electrolyte chemistry and electrode reaction pathways. To the best of the authors' knowledge, this is the first study addressing fuel-assisted operation in MCECs. In this context, the present work aims not only to assess the feasibility of this operating mode, but also to provide insight into the underlying electrochemical behavior and reaction mechanisms. Unlike SOECs, CO <sub>2</sub> in MCECs is generated internally regardless of the anodic inlet gas composition, introducing an additional level of complexity in the implementation of fuel-assisted strategies. The objective of this work is to investigate whether MCECs can operate in FA mode, to identify the associated challenges, and to elucidate the reactions involved. From a purely thermodynamic and stoichiometric standpoint, the methane flow rate supplied to the anode (10 Nml/min) would theoretically allow fuel-assisted operation up to current densities of about 450 mA/cm <sup>2</sup>. However, this condition is not observed experimentally. This discrepancy indicates that, although methane electrochemical oxidation occurs at low current densities, its kinetics become increasingly unfavorable compared to oxygen evolution at higher current densities. In addition, at high current densities methane may not have sufficient time to effectively interact with the electrochemically generated oxygen, suggesting the onset of mass-transport and fluid-dynamic limitations. This effect is likely enhanced by the very low gas residence times of the present setup. Conversely, this behavior is not observed when H <sub>2</sub> is used as the anodic fuel, since hydrogen, being significantly more reactive than methane, can be fully oxidized over the investigated current density range.

## 4\. Conclusion

A key challenge for the broader development of high-temperature electrolysis technologies is the reduction of electrical energy consumption, which strongly impacts the overall efficiency and economic viability of hydrogen production. This study aims to explore fuel-assisted operation in MCECs, a strategy pursued to reduce the operating voltage and improve the overall energy efficiency of high-temperature electrolysis. The investigation focuses on evaluating the performance of MCECs when hydrogen or methane is supplied as the anodic fuel. The experimental campaign was structured in two stages: first, tests were performed on a small-scale button cell to assess the fundamental feasibility and electrochemical behavior; subsequently, a larger planar cell was employed to validate the findings and allow detailed gas analysis at the anode outlet.

Results from the button cell indicate that at low current densities, methane can actively participate in anodic reactions, contributing to a measurable decrease in cell voltage. As the current density exceeds 100 mA/cm <sup>2</sup>, however, oxygen evolution increasingly dominates, and the cell's behavior approaches that of conventional MCEC operation. This result suggests a lower reactivity of methane toward oxygen ions (O <sup>2−</sup>), particularly at higher current densities.

Tests with the planar cell confirmed that hydrogen can effectively enable fuel-assisted operation, validating the mechanism and reproducing the characteristic turning-point behavior. When methane was used, polarization curves alone were insufficient to directly quantify its conversion. Gas analysis at 700 °C revealed that oxygen evolution dominates at higher currents, while methane oxidation is limited and occurs predominantly at low current densities. Variations in inlet water content had negligible impact on the cell voltage and outlet anodic composition, indicating that under the tested conditions methane decomposition remains kinetically restricted.

Overall, these results demonstrate that fuel-assisted operation in MCECs is feasible with highly reactive fuels such as hydrogen, whereas methane utilization is strongly limited by its low reactivity under the investigated conditions. These findings provide a first mechanistic understanding of FA operation in MCECs and highlight that future improvements should focus on enhancing methane activation through the development of more active anodic materials and/or catalytic phases, as well as optimizing reactor design parameters such as reactant residence time to promote fuel conversion and enable effective operation with less reactive fuels.
