## 1\. Introduction

Today, [syngas production](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/syngas-generation) is accompanied by greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions due to high heating requirements, sides-reactions, and [fugitive emissions](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/fugitive-emission) associated with natural gas conversion via [steam methane reforming](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/methane-steam-reforming) or [autothermal reforming](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/autothermal-reforming). 2:1 H <sub>2</sub>:CO syngas is specifically required for fuel production, including [Fischer Tropsch Synthesis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/fischer-tropsch-synthesis) (FTS) (18 Mt/year) and [methanol synthesis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/methanol-synthesis) (32 Mt/year),. 2:1 syngas can be produced from Steam Methane Reforming (SMR), Autothermal Reforming (ATR), [Partial Oxidation of Methane](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/partial-oxidation-of-methane) (POX) in various combinations,. Despite commercial operation, these processes have shortcomings, including CO <sub>2</sub> production and a costly need for [air purification](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/air-purification) to provide pure oxygen,.

Two leading approaches to reduce emissions in 2:1 [syngas production](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/chemical-engineering/syngas-generation) include using (1) CO <sub>2</sub> as a feedstock via [dry reforming](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/dry-reforming) (reaction ) with a supplemental [hydrogen source](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/hydrogen-source), or (2) low-CO <sub>2</sub> electricity via electrolysis of CO <sub>2</sub> and H <sub>2</sub> O,,,. Recently, developments in methane pyrolysis have led to interest in it as a way to produce CO <sub>2</sub> -free hydrogen. None of the alternative processes have reached commercial scale; however, the economics favor CO <sub>2</sub> abatement costs and low-cost, low-GHG electricity. The motivation of this work has been to understand what the most economically viable syngas production method would be in light of many recent developments, and in locations with CO <sub>2</sub> penalties, and low-CO <sub>2</sub> electricity.

In principle dry methane reforming (DMR, reaction ) mitigates many of the environmental shortcomings of syngas production; however, the resulting 1:1 H <sub>2</sub>:CO syngas is hydrogen lean and requires supplemental hydrogen. In addition, coking deactivates most dry reforming catalysts with only a few notable exceptions mitigated by interface-confined catalyst and metal-support interaction,.

Methane pyrolysis (PY, reaction ) has recently been proposed in new catalytic processes because it produces CO <sub>2</sub> -free hydrogen,,,,,,,. If combined with dry reforming (DMR, reaction ) in a molten metal bubble column, the resulting products are 2:1 syngas and solid carbon (reaction ). In this process, solid carbon floats to the surface of a bubble column where it could be continuously removed and sold or stored in perpetuity,,. In addition, molten metal bubble columns have high rates of heat transfer, which allows for affordable use of electric heating. To-date, only 4 catalysts have been investigated for this combination of reactions, and NiIn was reported as an active catalyst for both pyrolysis and CO <sub>2</sub> reforming; however, kinetic data for the two reactions is required to analyze and compare this process to state-of-the-art and emerging processes. Kinetic data for use in a reactor or process model has not been reported to date.(1)(2)(3)

This paper compares the above proposed process to other processes, 3 of which are detailed here. The first is the combination of steam methane reforming with dry reforming to achieve 2:1 syngas. As early as 1993, a dry reforming study done by Rostrup Nielsen gave the possibility that combining the Steaming Methane Reforming (reaction ) and Dry Methane Reforming (reaction ) to produce 2:1 H <sub>2</sub>:CO syngas (reaction, SMR/DMR).(4)(5)(6)

The second comparative case is autothermal reforming (ATR), which is a mature and cost-effective syngas production technology and has been used commercially for 2:1 syngas. While there are multiple commercialized processes for 2:1 syngas production, ATR is used herein as the state-of-art method. The reaction involves partial oxidation of methane and [steam reforming](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/steam-reforming),. The ratio of syngas produced is usually 1.5–3 and can be adjusted by changing the ratio of reactants. After improvements by Topsoe, the technology has been widely used in gas-to-liquid plants.

This work also looks at the production of 2:1 syngas via electrolysis. The combination of CO <sub>2</sub> and [water electrolysis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/water-electrolysis) is used such that the feedstock only consists of water and CO <sub>2</sub>. The produced syngas ratio can be varied by changing the feed ratio between water and CO <sub>2</sub> (reaction ). Significant research efforts have been devoted to solid oxide [electrolysis cells](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/electrolysis-cell), and their long-term stable performance, particularly in conjunction with nuclear energy. Low-CO <sub>2</sub> electricity is directly utilized to perform the electrolysis reactions, and so this technology is used for comparison to the above processes.(7)

Herein, the two dry-reforming-based methods and electrolysis are compared to commercial autothermal reforming from economic and environmental perspectives. Side-by-side process and economic models are established and optimized in order to show the techno-economic framework for each case and also compared with previous studies,. Electrified solutions for each case are considered for consideration in environments with low-CO <sub>2</sub> electricity. The techno-economic analysis of combining methane pyrolysis and dry reforming, PY/DMR, has not been reported to date. Experimentally determined kinetic data is presented and used to size the PY/DMR reactor and optimize the process.

## 2\. Experimental

Three main processes for syngas production are analyzed in this study and compared to the commercial [ATR](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/autothermal-reforming). In order to best compare the processes, SMR/DMR, PY/DMR, co-electrolysis, and ATR processes are compared with the same assumptions, scope, scales, and economic environments. PY/DMR and co-electrolysis are heated electrically in all scenarios, while both electric and [fired heaters](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/fired-heaters) are considered for SMR/DMR.

### 2.1. Dry reforming + methane pyrolysis (PY/DMR)

Our recently reported developments in molten metal catalyzed pyrolysis and dry reforming utilize a bubble column reactor, and this paper specifically looks at the case of utilizing low-CO <sub>2</sub> electricity to provide the heat of reaction electrically. In addition, a detailed design for a molten metal bubble column reactor heated by [fossil fuel combustion](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/fossil-fuel-combustion) has not been reported. For these reasons, only electrically heated PY/DMR is considered in all cases, despite the fact that at lower CO <sub>2</sub> costs of mitigation, fossil fuel combustion might offer economic benefits. This is different from the case of SMR/DMR, where both electric and fired heater reactors are well known, and in this paper, the lowest cost option is used for any given scenario.

In PY/DMR, one of the dominant factors in conversion is the operating pressure, because DMR has higher equilibrium conversion at low pressure. However, lower pressure results in higher volume unit operations and lower reactant [partial pressures](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/partial-pressure), resulting in a lower rate. The optimum was determined to be 10 bar which provides 92.5 % methane and 97.6 % CO <sub>2</sub> conversion. Fig. A.1(a) is the [thermodynamic equilibrium](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/thermodynamic-equilibrium) plot generated by FactSage, and shows the negative effect of pressure on the reaction at the equilibrium state. In the combined PY/DMR situation with a 2:1 CH <sub>4</sub> to CO <sub>2</sub> feed ratio, the conversion of methane keeps decreasing and producing more water with elevated pressure in the equilibrium.

The electrified methane reactor is costed from previous work. The major cost of a resistive heater is the [resistive element](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/resistive-element) which depends on its energy consumption. Therefore, adequate preheating is necessary in order to minimize energy consumption from the resistive heater and reduce its unit cost. CO <sub>2</sub> is assumed to be taken from CO <sub>2</sub> capture plant at pipeline pressure, and therefore at 30 bar at 25 ℃, while methane is taken from a natural gas [wellhead](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/wellhead) at 30 bar and 25 ℃. It is not viable to preheating the feedstock over 800 ℃ due to the premature reactions. The gases are preheated and expanded to 800 ℃ and 10 bar using recovered heat. After preheating with a series of heat exchangers, the reaction is carried out in a bubble column reactor. The cost of the insulation and vessel section of the reactor is scaled by their relative size. Conversely, the cost of the resistive element is scaled by the duty of the reaction. The produced crude syngas is sent to an amine absorber and stripper to ensure the concentration of CO <sub>2</sub> in the purified syngas is lower than 20 ppm.

### 2.2. Autothermal reforming (ATR)

The essential raw materials for ATR are water, methane, and oxygen. Usually, the outlet temperature is around 900–1100 °C with the oxygen to methane ratio around 0.55–0.60,. The steam to carbon ratio (S/C) in the raw material varies depending on the reactor configuration and catalyst used. Some unwanted side reactions such as soot formation could transpire if the steam to carbon ratio is too low. By adjusting the steam feed ratio, the ratio of syngas produced can also be adjusted. The upper part is the [combustion zone](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/combustion-zone), where exothermic [methane partial oxidation](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/methane-partial-oxidation) occurs, Eq. Oxygen is assumed to be purchased as gaseous oxygen, with a temperature at 25 ℃ and supply pressure at 20 bar, respectively, from the state-of-the-arts air separation unit from Linde. For these reasons, ATR as an existing commercial process is a suitable benchmark reference to compare with other processes. In order to make consistency with other scenario, the products also entered the amine absorber/stripper system and remove most of the CO <sub>2</sub> produced.

### 2.3. Dry reforming + Steam reforming (SMR/DMR)

The SMR/DMR model includes one combined reactor, for both [SMR](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/methane-steam-reforming) and DMR, and the [effluent streams](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/effluent-stream) are combined to produce 2:1 H <sub>2</sub>:CO syngas. SMR is a well-known and cost-efficient approach to produce high-ratio syngas. One limitation is the associated CO <sub>2</sub> emitted during the reforming process. It usually produces 9–10 kg of CO <sub>2</sub> per kilogram of [hydrogen production](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/hydrogen-production). The equilibrium conversion gets hurt massively from the increased pressure. The suggested operating pressure and temperature is 5–30 bar and 800 – 950 ℃, respectively, that verified experimentally by Olah et al. In the thermodynamic standpoint, 5 bar is the highest pressure to achieve 90 % conversion and will be used in this study. The equilibrium plot versus pressure is shown in A.1(b). DMR is challenged by coking. Nick based catalyst, for example 15-NiO-MgO, is usually used with the loading rate at 0.16 kg/ *t* -syngas. The catalyst for SMR/DMR and ATR are replaced once a year for a conservative estimation and the base case catalyst price is $21/lbs. In bi-reforming reaction, high pipeline pressure methane and CO <sub>2</sub> expand from 30 bar to 5 bar and mixed. In our process model, the product stream flows out of the bi-reforming reactor is at 950 ℃. Both SMR and DMR are [endothermic reactions](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/endothermic-reaction), so it also needs an extra source of heat. The [thermal energy](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/thermal-energy) supply to the fired heater reactor is assumed to be burning natural gas with air at 80 % thermal efficiency.

The stream composition of bi-reforming reaction also is calculated by Gibbs free reactor. In the base-case, a fired heater is used to provide the endothermic heat of reaction because it results in a lower cost process. The cost of the reformer-type fired heater reactor are estimated by the duty of the reactions. However, under other economic environments considered, electrically heated SMR/DMR is considered. In all scenarios, both heating methods are considered, and the lowest syngas-sale price method is used.

### 2.4. CO2 and H2O co-electrolysis

The last process is an electrochemical way to produce valuable fuel from CO <sub>2</sub>, namely CO <sub>2</sub> and H <sub>2</sub> O co-electrolysis. The reactant includes only water and CO <sub>2</sub>, which makes it a green process. Solid oxide [electrolysis cells](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/electrolysis-cell) have been proved to be a reliable method to produce syngas via CO <sub>2</sub> co-electrolysis at high temperatures (around 700–800°) more than ten years ago.

Part of this process is adapted from previous work where CO <sub>2</sub> co-electrolysis is conducted in a solid oxide [isothermal](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/isothermal) [electrolyser](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/electrolyser) at 800 ℃. Hence, preheating is necessary. Compared with low-pressure electrolysis in the previous work, high-pressure electrolysis has been gradually realized in recent years. [Electrolytic cells](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/electrolytic-cell) with an operating pressure of 30 bar are used here. So, we also assume that the [discharge pressure](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/discharge-pressure) is 30 bar, which is also a good match for the pressure required for the final product. The extra revenue from selling the coproduct oxygen is discussed in the sensitivity analysis. The oxygen produced is sold at room temperature and pressure state.

Recently, researchers have been trying to analyze the feasibility from both technological and economic standpoints. It has been shown that with the incline of the cost of CO <sub>2</sub> abatement, some valuable chemicals produced from CO <sub>2</sub> co-electrolysis are even had better economic competitive than any state-of-arts today.

### 2.5. Process design assumptions and economic basis

The simulated processes are assumed to be 500 kilo metric tonnes of 2:1 ratio syngas per year (500kta) sized plants. The plant's life is 20 years, and the plant's operating and maintenance costs are taken into account. All the schemes are simulated by Aspen HYSYS V12. The Peng-Robinson fluid package is used for most of the calculations, whereas the amine absorber and stripper sections use Acid Gas-Chemical Solvents fluid package.

The processes using CO <sub>2</sub> and natural gas are assumed to be realistic composition at well-head pressure (30 bar) and atmospheric temperature. The detailed composition of CO <sub>2</sub> and natural gas are listed in the. Water is supplied at room temperature and atmospheric pressure, that is, 25°Celsius and 1 atm. The overall simulation analysis process includes three parts: feedstocks pre-treatment before reaction, reaction and post-reaction treatment. Preheating the raw material in terms of raising/lowering pressure and preheat the feedstocks are included before the reaction. The reactions take place at high temperatures, so for the reactions that has water in the effluent, namely SMR/DMR and ATR, the process stream is cooled down to condense the water. An amine absorber/stripper system is used to absorb and separate any CO <sub>2</sub>. Finally, an appropriate proportion of syngas (2:1) is slightly heated to 200 ℃ and 30 bar to achieve the conditions required for further downstream plant, low temperature [Fischer Tropsch Synthesis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/fischer-tropsch-synthesis) (LT-FTS) reaction,.

All prices in 2020 was used as the basis for calculation (i.e., CEPCI 2020). We assume that the reactor and process is used for 20 years. It is worth mentioned that, in the above processes, there is sometimes a large temperature rise during the preheating stage. All the heaters during each heating path represents a series of continuous heaters or heat exchangers. The overall heater/cooler configuration and utility consumption detailed in the heat integration section in. Because there are many heating/cooling processes in the process that can be integrated, for example, high-temperature products coming out of the reactor can provide energy for pre-heating section that prior to the reaction. Therefore, all heating and cooling processes are strictly involved in heat integration model by Aspen Energy Analyzer <sup>@</sup> for energy-saving and economic purposes. This heat integration model also provides the heat exchanger’s cost. The cost of all the conventional equipment is generated by CAPCOST except resistive heater and high temperature cyclone in PY/DMR and electrolyser in co-electrolysis scenario. The pressure devices include pumps, compressors and expanders. The cost of pressure devices depends on their power and discharge pressure. The cost of fired heater reactor depends on the energy consumption.

Since ATR is a mature process that is commercially available, we assume that the [internal rate of return](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/internal-rate-of-return) (IRR) is 10 % as a base case. SMR/DMR and PY/DMR have not been put into commercial operation and might have a higher risk and some unplanned expenditure due to the technology's immaturity. Similar to SMR/DMR, [water electrolysis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/water-electrolysis) is a well-discussed topic. The feasibility has been proved in many different aspects, such as via [photocatalysis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/chemical-engineering/photocatalysis) and photo-electrochemistry. The CO <sub>2</sub> co-electrolysis method has been used in some areas in the world. Hence, the IRR for both SMR/DMR and co-electrolysis are assumed to be 13 % as a moderate risk level technology. Compared to SMR/DMR and co-electrolysis, which are based on some extensions of matured SMR and water electrolysis, the PY/DMR we studied presents a higher risk. This process has not been discussed yet, and there are some challenges in applying molten catalyst to practical production. ## 3\. Results and discussion

### 3.1. Experimental results

In order to size the PY/DMR, a rate expression was used with experimentally determined pre-exponential, [activation energy](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/activation-energy), and reaction orders. The catalyst with the highest activity previously reported was molten Ni-In alloy with a mole composition of 65:35 Ni:In, which has been used as the basis for this work. However, previously, there was insufficient kinetic information to put into a [kinetic model](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/kinetic-model), so further experiments are reported herein. Pyrolysis (reaction ) and dry methane reforming (reaction ) parameters were measured separately by operating the reactor under conditions in which only one of the two reaction was observable.

Reactions, occurred in different temperature regions, where dry reforming occurred at lower temperatures than pyrolysis. In our reactor system, methane pyrolysis was not detected below 955 °C while dry reforming of methane was observed at all temperatures above 900 °C. Therefore, the [kinetics parameters](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/kinetic-parameter) for each reaction could be measured independently. Specifically, at 960 °C, only methane and argon were feed into the reactor to test the reaction order of methane in pyrolysis, whereas, at 950 °C, CH <sub>4</sub>, CO <sub>2</sub> and argon were co-feed to test the reaction orders of dry reforming (a). In all the experiments, the overall [gas flow rate](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/gas-flowrate) is constant (10 sccm), and argon is co-feed to balance the flow rate and maintain constant residence time. The linear fit lines indicate the reaction order is one for CH <sub>4</sub> in both pyrolysis and DMR reaction.

Similarly, the reverse reactions were measured by feeding the products only (b), because in order to include all the kinetic data into a reactor and process model, the reaction order of products is also required. We also report the case of a carbon price of negative $20/t (i.e., paying to sequester the carbon in the ground), if the type and quality of the collected carbon is not suitable for commercial use and market requirement. In this case, PY/DMR is much more expensive with a $271.1/t sale price.

## 4\. Conclusion

Syngas production technologies were modeled in the context of making the best use of low-CO <sub>2</sub> electricity in economic environments with a cost of CO <sub>2</sub> abatement. British Columbia, Canada was used as the location due to the existing affordable [hydro power](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/hydro-power) ($0.0537/kWh and 12.9 g-CO <sub>2</sub> /kWh) and cost of CO <sub>2</sub> abatement ($50/t). Five routes producing 2:1 ratio syngas was modeled and compared in detailed: ATR SMR/DMR, SMR/DMR-ele, CO <sub>2</sub> co-electrolysis and PY/DMR. Water electrolysis/rWGS and electricity powered SMR/DMR were also discussed to make a comparison. The minimum syngas sale price of nth-of-a-kind PY/DMR will be 204.6/t, which is $10.9 higher than convention ATR.

For PY/DMR, activation energies, pre-exponential factors, and reaction orders were experimentally determined for both methane pyrolysis and CO <sub>2</sub> reforming to enable a new method of producing syngas using a molten Ni-In catalyst in an electrically heated bubble column reactor to produce 2:1 syngas and solid carbon in one reactor. The activation energy for methane pyrolysis and DMR were determined using [Arrhenius plots](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/arrhenius-plot) to be 187 kJ/mol and 99 kJ/mol, respectively. In addition, both pyrolysis and DMR were consistent with [first order reaction](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/first-order-reaction) kinetics, and the exponent of both H <sub>2</sub> and CO in reverse DMR reaction is 2, H <sub>2</sub> in reverse pyrolysis reaction is 1.

When upstream emission, reaction by-products, CO <sub>2</sub> from electricity generation, O <sub>2</sub> or CO <sub>2</sub> preparation and [flue gas](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/flue-gas) emission are taken into account, PY/DMR and co-electrolysis are the only syngas production processes that result in net negative CO <sub>2eq</sub> emissions. For every 1000 g of 2:1 synthesis gas containing produced from PY/DMR, 0.329 kg of CO <sub>2eq</sub> is consumed, and 1.06 kg for co-electrolysis. In contrast, ATR and SMR/DMR produce 390 g and 356 g, respectively. The levelized cost of CO <sub>2</sub> mitigation of PY/DMR is 60/t-CO <sub>2eq</sub>, which is cheaper than the conventional CCS technologies. Co-electrolysis is $152.3/t-CO <sub>2eq</sub>.

Based on the economic model developed in the base case and the range of reasonable electricity and natural gas prices, SMR/DMR is the most economically competitive scheme combining SMR and if a stable catalyst is used for DMR. Assuming that NPVs are 0, the minimum syngas sale prices of ATR, SMR/DMR, PY/DMR and CO <sub>2</sub> co-electrolysis are $193.7, $179.2, $222 and $393.2/t, respectively. Sensitivity to costs, global location of plant citing, and CO <sub>2</sub> associated with electricity were investigated. PY/DMR would be the favored technology when the electricity price is less than $0.029/kWh, carbon is sold at more than $377/t or cost of CO <sub>2</sub> abatement is higher than $104/t. The only sensitivity factors were found to result in co-electrolysis being the most economically favorable technology in the ranges studied is that the green electricity price less than $0.021/kWh or the carbon abatement cost is over $305/t. Under the 2050 net-zero emission scenario, PY/DMR would achieve the best economic competitiveness, and the projected syngas price is $91/t.

## 2\. Experimental

Three main processes for syngas production are analyzed in this study and compared to the commercial [ATR](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/autothermal-reforming). ### 2.1. Dry reforming + methane pyrolysis (PY/DMR)

Our recently reported developments in molten metal catalyzed pyrolysis and dry reforming utilize a bubble column reactor, and this paper specifically looks at the case of utilizing low-CO <sub>2</sub> electricity to provide the heat of reaction electrically. In addition, a detailed design for a molten metal bubble column reactor heated by [fossil fuel combustion](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/fossil-fuel-combustion) has not been reported. For these reasons, only electrically heated PY/DMR is considered in all cases, despite the fact that at lower CO <sub>2</sub> costs of mitigation, fossil fuel combustion might offer economic benefits. This is different from the case of SMR/DMR, where both electric and fired heater reactors are well known, and in this paper, the lowest cost option is used for any given scenario.

In PY/DMR, one of the dominant factors in conversion is the operating pressure, because DMR has higher equilibrium conversion at low pressure. However, lower pressure results in higher volume unit operations and lower reactant [partial pressures](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/partial-pressure), resulting in a lower rate. The optimum was determined to be 10 bar which provides 92.5 % methane and 97.6 % CO <sub>2</sub> conversion. Fig. A.1(a) is the [thermodynamic equilibrium](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/thermodynamic-equilibrium) plot generated by FactSage, and shows the negative effect of pressure on the reaction at the equilibrium state. In the combined PY/DMR situation with a 2:1 CH <sub>4</sub> to CO <sub>2</sub> feed ratio, the conversion of methane keeps decreasing and producing more water with elevated pressure in the equilibrium.

The electrified methane reactor is costed from previous work. The major cost of a resistive heater is the [resistive element](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/resistive-element) which depends on its energy consumption. Therefore, adequate preheating is necessary in order to minimize energy consumption from the resistive heater and reduce its unit cost. CO <sub>2</sub> is assumed to be taken from CO <sub>2</sub> capture plant at pipeline pressure, and therefore at 30 bar at 25 ℃, while methane is taken from a natural gas [wellhead](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/wellhead) at 30 bar and 25 ℃. It is not viable to preheating the feedstock over 800 ℃ due to the premature reactions. The gases are preheated and expanded to 800 ℃ and 10 bar using recovered heat. After preheating with a series of heat exchangers, the reaction is carried out in a bubble column reactor. The cost of the insulation and vessel section of the reactor is scaled by their relative size. Conversely, the cost of the resistive element is scaled by the duty of the reaction. The produced crude syngas is sent to an amine absorber and stripper to ensure the concentration of CO <sub>2</sub> in the purified syngas is lower than 20 ppm.

### 2.3. Dry reforming + Steam reforming (SMR/DMR)

The SMR/DMR model includes one combined reactor, for both [SMR](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/methane-steam-reforming) and DMR, and the [effluent streams](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/effluent-stream) are combined to produce 2:1 H <sub>2</sub>:CO syngas. SMR is a well-known and cost-efficient approach to produce high-ratio syngas. One limitation is the associated CO <sub>2</sub> emitted during the reforming process. It usually produces 9–10 kg of CO <sub>2</sub> per kilogram of [hydrogen production](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/hydrogen-production). The equilibrium conversion gets hurt massively from the increased pressure. The suggested operating pressure and temperature is 5–30 bar and 800 – 950 ℃, respectively, that verified experimentally by Olah et al. In the thermodynamic standpoint, 5 bar is the highest pressure to achieve 90 % conversion and will be used in this study. The equilibrium plot versus pressure is shown in A.1(b). DMR is challenged by coking. Nick based catalyst, for example 15-NiO-MgO, is usually used with the loading rate at 0.16 kg/ *t* -syngas. The catalyst for SMR/DMR and ATR are replaced once a year for a conservative estimation and the base case catalyst price is $21/lbs. In bi-reforming reaction, high pipeline pressure methane and CO <sub>2</sub> expand from 30 bar to 5 bar and mixed. In our process model, the product stream flows out of the bi-reforming reactor is at 950 ℃. Both SMR and DMR are [endothermic reactions](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/endothermic-reaction), so it also needs an extra source of heat. The [thermal energy](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/thermal-energy) supply to the fired heater reactor is assumed to be burning natural gas with air at 80 % thermal efficiency.

The stream composition of bi-reforming reaction also is calculated by Gibbs free reactor. In the base-case, a fired heater is used to provide the endothermic heat of reaction because it results in a lower cost process. The cost of the reformer-type fired heater reactor are estimated by the duty of the reactions. However, under other economic environments considered, electrically heated SMR/DMR is considered. In all scenarios, both heating methods are considered, and the lowest syngas-sale price method is used.

### 2.4. CO2 and H2O co-electrolysis

The last process is an electrochemical way to produce valuable fuel from CO <sub>2</sub>, namely CO <sub>2</sub> and H <sub>2</sub> O co-electrolysis. The reactant includes only water and CO <sub>2</sub>, which makes it a green process. Solid oxide [electrolysis cells](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/electrolysis-cell) have been proved to be a reliable method to produce syngas via CO <sub>2</sub> co-electrolysis at high temperatures (around 700–800°) more than ten years ago.

Part of this process is adapted from previous work where CO <sub>2</sub> co-electrolysis is conducted in a solid oxide [isothermal](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/isothermal) [electrolyser](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/electrolyser) at 800 ℃. Hence, preheating is necessary. Compared with low-pressure electrolysis in the previous work, high-pressure electrolysis has been gradually realized in recent years. [Electrolytic cells](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/electrolytic-cell) with an operating pressure of 30 bar are used here. So, we also assume that the [discharge pressure](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/discharge-pressure) is 30 bar, which is also a good match for the pressure required for the final product. The extra revenue from selling the coproduct oxygen is discussed in the sensitivity analysis. The oxygen produced is sold at room temperature and pressure state.

Recently, researchers have been trying to analyze the feasibility from both technological and economic standpoints. It has been shown that with the incline of the cost of CO <sub>2</sub> abatement, some valuable chemicals produced from CO <sub>2</sub> co-electrolysis are even had better economic competitive than any state-of-arts today.

### 2.5. Process design assumptions and economic basis

The simulated processes are assumed to be 500 kilo metric tonnes of 2:1 ratio syngas per year (500kta) sized plants. ### 3.1. Experimental results

In order to size the PY/DMR, a rate expression was used with experimentally determined pre-exponential, [activation energy](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/activation-energy), and reaction orders. The catalyst with the highest activity previously reported was molten Ni-In alloy with a mole composition of 65:35 Ni:In, which has been used as the basis for this work. However, previously, there was insufficient kinetic information to put into a [kinetic model](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/kinetic-model), so further experiments are reported herein. Pyrolysis (reaction ) and dry methane reforming (reaction ) parameters were measured separately by operating the reactor under conditions in which only one of the two reaction was observable.

Reactions, occurred in different temperature regions, where dry reforming occurred at lower temperatures than pyrolysis. In our reactor system, methane pyrolysis was not detected below 955 °C while dry reforming of methane was observed at all temperatures above 900 °C. Therefore, the [kinetics parameters](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/kinetic-parameter) for each reaction could be measured independently. Specifically, at 960 °C, only methane and argon were feed into the reactor to test the reaction order of methane in pyrolysis, whereas, at 950 °C, CH <sub>4</sub>, CO <sub>2</sub> and argon were co-feed to test the reaction orders of dry reforming (a). In all the experiments, the overall [gas flow rate](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/gas-flowrate) is constant (10 sccm), and argon is co-feed to balance the flow rate and maintain constant residence time. The linear fit lines indicate the reaction order is one for CH <sub>4</sub> in both pyrolysis and DMR reaction.

### 3.2. Process modelling

#### 3.2.1. Dry reforming + methane pyrolysis (PY/DMR)

The process model developed for PY/DMR is shown in. Aspen HYSYS is used to simulate a 500-kta syngas production plant. The [plug flow reactor](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/plug-flow-reactor) with the tested experimental data was used. Experimental results are exhibited in Section 3.7. Independent power-law [kinetic equations](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/kinetic-equation) for methane pyrolysis and dry reforming are be applied in the process model in order to size the reaction. We assume that the gas volume in the bubbles to the total reactor volume is 0.25, as with used in the previous works,,. Furthermore, the average [bubble diameter](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/bubble-diameter) is assumed to be 1 cm, as used in the previous work. For each bubble, the surface-to-volume ratio is 0.5/π. In order to generate 500-kta syngas, the estimated reactor volume required for 90 % (in order to match the equilibrium conversion of SMR/DMR) CH <sub>4</sub> conversion is 236 m <sup>3</sup>. A previous optimization-based study has proved that the [isothermal condition](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/isothermal-condition) can be achieved when a bubble column reactor with internal diameter of 4.4 m is used. We assume that the PY/DMR reaction is conducted in three parallel reactors with 5.2 m height under the optimal condition. The catalyst consisted of 73 % Nickel and 27 % [Indium](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/chemical-engineering/indium). The heat source of the reactor is an electric resistive heater with a capital cost of $13.56 m in total. An [electric arc furnace](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/electric-arc-furnace) was also considered but would result in higher capex.

In the process model, the produced solid carbon is separated in a high temperature conventional cyclone. According to the reports from EIA, the current average natural gas price is $4/GJ,. We consider the scenarios where the price of natural gas rises or falls by 25 % to become $3/GJ or $5/GJ. SMR/DMR still has the lowest sale prices of $206.2/t at the maximum NG rate. It can be seen that when the natural gas price rises to $5/GJ, PY/DMR is still less competitive than ATR ($250.9/t & $220.3/t). The lowest syngas sale price based on $3/GJ natural gas are $152.2/t, $193.3/t, $167.1/t for SMR/DMR, PY/DMR and ATR, respectively. The ATR reaction is the scheme that consumes the most natural gas. Hence, if gas prices rise, ATR also suffers the most. SMR/DMR is the most competitive solution, regardless of how natural gas price changes.

PY/DMR and co-electrolysis are impacted by the sale of coproduced solid carbon and oxygen. In the base case, the solid carbon is assumed to have a value equivalent to low-grade [metallurgical coke](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/metallurgical-coke) with a price of $150 per tonne. Currently, moderate purity coke from petroleum that is used in metallurgy is around $250/t. If the sale price of solid carbon is increased to $400 per tonne, the annual revenue from selling solid carbon rises from $14.1 m to $37.7 m. It would successfully reduce the price of syngas by $47.1 per tonne and reach $174.9/ *t* -syngas. In this situation, PY/DMR becomes the lowest cost solution. If the price of solid carbon is $377/t, PY/DMR is the lowest cost process.

Besides the metallurgical coke, carbon black accounting for up to 60 percent of the solid carbon production. It is a more valuable product with a sale price of around $0.58/lb, that mostly used for rubber tire production. Recent research has proved that the produced solid carbon is in carbon black phase. If PY/DMR produced carbon black, an additional $58.2 m of revenue would be generated annually through efficient production and separation of black carbon. This would reduce the minimum sale price of syngas by $116.4/t to $105.6 per tonne, resulting in the lowest cost production method.

We also report the case of a carbon price of negative $20/t (i.e., paying to sequester the carbon in the ground), if the type and quality of the collected carbon is not suitable for commercial use and market requirement. In this case, PY/DMR is much more expensive with a $271.1/t sale price.

Selling high purity oxygen gas from co-electrolysis at $120/t (50 % higher) also help it significantly in the economic aspect. It reduces the syngas price down more than $50/t, from $387.6 to 333.3/t, but it is still less economically competitive than all the hydrocarbon-based technologies.

In addition, we investigate the effect of catalyst cost. Ni-In is used as the base case as it is the best performing catalyst to-date; however, we also look at two other cases. Based on the nickel and indium purchase cost that mentioned in the previous section, the catalyst's purchase price is about $372,000/m <sup>3</sup>. Ni-Bi has been approved for a suitable candidate for methane pyrolysis. If the reaction rate and reactor volume (i.e., the amount of catalyst) do not change, using 27 %Ni-73 %Bi instead of Ni-In catalysts reduces the catalyst purchase cost from $65.8 m to $11.6 m. It reduces the syngas sale price by $22.7 per tonne, to $199.3/t. Under this assumption, the cost savings would be even greater if the iron catalyst could be used as an alternative. The current price of [Iron ore](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/chemical-engineering/iron-ore) is around $130 per tonne, and the use of an iron catalyst would reduce the price of syngas by $27.5 per tonne. Therefore, a new catalyst for this reaction would result in significant cost savings.

## 4\. Conclusion

Syngas production technologies were modeled in the context of making the best use of low-CO <sub>2</sub> electricity in economic environments with a cost of CO <sub>2</sub> abatement. British Columbia, Canada was used as the location due to the existing affordable [hydro power](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/hydro-power) ($0.0537/kWh and 12.9 g-CO <sub>2</sub> /kWh) and cost of CO <sub>2</sub> abatement ($50/t). Five routes producing 2:1 ratio syngas was modeled and compared in detailed: ATR SMR/DMR, SMR/DMR-ele, CO <sub>2</sub> co-electrolysis and PY/DMR. Water electrolysis/rWGS and electricity powered SMR/DMR were also discussed to make a comparison. The minimum syngas sale price of nth-of-a-kind PY/DMR will be 204.6/t, which is $10.9 higher than convention ATR.

For PY/DMR, activation energies, pre-exponential factors, and reaction orders were experimentally determined for both methane pyrolysis and CO <sub>2</sub> reforming to enable a new method of producing syngas using a molten Ni-In catalyst in an electrically heated bubble column reactor to produce 2:1 syngas and solid carbon in one reactor. The activation energy for methane pyrolysis and DMR were determined using [Arrhenius plots](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/arrhenius-plot) to be 187 kJ/mol and 99 kJ/mol, respectively. In addition, both pyrolysis and DMR were consistent with [first order reaction](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/first-order-reaction) kinetics, and the exponent of both H <sub>2</sub> and CO in reverse DMR reaction is 2, H <sub>2</sub> in reverse pyrolysis reaction is 1.

When upstream emission, reaction by-products, CO <sub>2</sub> from electricity generation, O <sub>2</sub> or CO <sub>2</sub> preparation and [flue gas](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/flue-gas) emission are taken into account, PY/DMR and co-electrolysis are the only syngas production processes that result in net negative CO <sub>2eq</sub> emissions. For every 1000 g of 2:1 synthesis gas containing produced from PY/DMR, 0.329 kg of CO <sub>2eq</sub> is consumed, and 1.06 kg for co-electrolysis. In contrast, ATR and SMR/DMR produce 390 g and 356 g, respectively. The levelized cost of CO <sub>2</sub> mitigation of PY/DMR is 60/t-CO <sub>2eq</sub>, which is cheaper than the conventional CCS technologies. Co-electrolysis is $152.3/t-CO <sub>2eq</sub>.

Based on the economic model developed in the base case and the range of reasonable electricity and natural gas prices, SMR/DMR is the most economically competitive scheme combining SMR and if a stable catalyst is used for DMR. Assuming that NPVs are 0, the minimum syngas sale prices of ATR, SMR/DMR, PY/DMR and CO <sub>2</sub> co-electrolysis are $193.7, $179.2, $222 and $393.2/t, respectively. Sensitivity to costs, global location of plant citing, and CO <sub>2</sub> associated with electricity were investigated. PY/DMR would be the favored technology when the electricity price is less than $0.029/kWh, carbon is sold at more than $377/t or cost of CO <sub>2</sub> abatement is higher than $104/t. The only sensitivity factors were found to result in co-electrolysis being the most economically favorable technology in the ranges studied is that the green electricity price less than $0.021/kWh or the carbon abatement cost is over $305/t. Under the 2050 net-zero emission scenario, PY/DMR would achieve the best economic competitiveness, and the projected syngas price is $91/t.
