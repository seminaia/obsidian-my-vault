## 1\. Introduction

In recent years, technology that uses CO <sub>2</sub> as a carbon resource has been attracting attention as a way to achieve a decarbonized society. One of the promising technologies for this is co-electrolysis of CO <sub>2</sub> H <sub>2</sub> O using a [solid oxide electrolysis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/solid-oxide-electrolysis) cell (SOEC). Co-electrolysis using SOEC can efficiently convert CO <sub>2</sub> into a mixture of CO and H <sub>2</sub>, which is known as [syngas](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/syngas) \[,,, \]. Syngas can be converted into valuable chemicals with [catalytic reaction](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/catalytic-reaction) like the Fischer-Tropsch (FT) or [methanation](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/methanation) synthesis \[,, \]. Therefore, the co-electrolysis using SOEC contributes to efficiently convert CO <sub>2</sub> into hydrocarbons or other chemicals for utilizing CO <sub>2</sub> as a carbon source. The [electrolytic cell](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/electrolytic-cell) is made of ceramics and has a structure similar to that of solid [oxide](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/materials-science/oxide-compound) fuel cell (SOFC) \[\]. Additionally, since SOEC operates at high temperatures, it can perform electrolysis at lower electrolytic voltages by utilizing heat, thus enabling the production of valuable chemicals with higher efficiency compared to low-temperature electrolysis methods such as alkaline [electrolysis cell](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/electrolysis-cell) (AEC) or [polymer electrolyte](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/materials-science/polymer-electrolyte) membrane electrolysis cell (PEMEC) \[,,\]. In contrast to traditional methods of producing [synthesis gas](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/materials-science/synthesis-gas) from [fossil fuels](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/materials-science/fossil-fuel), which have a large negative environmental impact by large greenhouse gas emissions \[\], highly efficient [electrochemical methods](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/electrochemical-method) such as co-electrolysis are more suitable for sustainable synthesis gas production \[\]. While co-electrolysis in SOECs possesses such attractive characteristics, the reaction mechanisms occurring on the electrodes are not yet fully understood since it is a relatively new technology compared to individual H <sub>2</sub> O electrolysis.

One of the major challenges of co-electrolysis is control of the composition of the products. This is because the ratio of H <sub>2</sub> to CO in the produced syngas should be suitable for the subsequent synthesis of valuable products. For example, a CO and H <sub>2</sub> ratio of 1:3 is required for methane production, 1:2 for Fischer-Tropsch synthesis or [methanol production](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/chemical-engineering/production-of-methanol), and 1:1 for oxygenated compound synthesis like methanol \[,\]. The main reactions in co-electrolysis are [electrochemical reduction](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/materials-science/electrochemical-reaction) of CO <sub>2</sub> and H <sub>2</sub> O. In addition to these main reactions, there are many side reactions included \[\]. Among the side reactions, reverse [water gas shift](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/chemical-engineering/water-gas-shift) (RWGS) reaction expressed by, which is a chemical reaction between the reactants and products of co-electrolysis, is significant effects on CO/H <sub>2</sub> ratio, \[\]. Among these reactions occur in combination with each other, the contribution of each reaction is complicated and unclear \[\]. Furthermore, the contributions of these reactions are varied depending on the cell materials, the structure of the cells and stacks, and various operational parameters \[\]. As a result, predicting and controlling the products becomes complex and difficult in CO <sub>2</sub> H <sub>2</sub> O co-electrolysis.(1)H <sub>2</sub> + CO <sub>2</sub> ⇌ CO + H <sub>2</sub> O

In previous studies on the reaction mechanism in co-electrolysis, two distinct mechanisms were initially reported. The first mechanism suggested that CO was primarily produced through the reverse [water gas shift reaction](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/water-gas-shift-reaction) (RWGS) between H₂ generated by H₂O electrolysis and CO₂. This hypothesis was suggested based on the observation that the ASR polarization resistance and the impedance in I-V curves for co-electrolysis showed almost the same values with those for H₂O electrolysis \[,,,, \]. Such results were mainly observed on the small disc shape cell \[,\] however similar results are also reported on the stack level \[,,, \]. The second mechanism proposed that CO₂ electrolysis was main route for CO production. This hypothesis arose because the ASR polarization resistance and impedance in I-V curves for co-electrolysis is intermediate value between H₂O and CO₂ electrolysis \[,, \]. This trend was observed not only in small cells \[,\] but also in large cells \[\]. In recent years, the influence of [RWGS](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/water-gas-shift) and CO₂ electrolysis on CO production has become more clearly understood with respect to operating conditions \[,,,, \]. For example, Dittrich et al. \[\], Ioannidou et al. \[\], and Wolf et al. \[\] evaluated the effects of feed gas composition on co-electrolysis in small cells with Ni [cermet](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/materials-science/cermet) electrodes. Their results suggested that CO₂ electrolysis became prominent when the concentration of supplied H₂O fell below a certain threshold. Furthermore, Zheng et al. \[\] demonstrated that, in co-electrolysis using a large single cell with Ni [cermet](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/cermet) electrodes, H₂O electrolysis was the primary reaction at [low current densities](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/low-current-density), while CO₂ electrolysis occurred at [high current densities](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/high-current-density). Similarly, Liang et al. investigated the effects of feed gas composition and current density on co-electrolysis using a large single cell and reported that the current density at which CO₂ electrolysis begins is changed depending on the feed gas composition \[\].

As discussed above, significant progress has been made in understanding the reaction mechanisms and the effects of operating parameters in co-electrolysis. However, these evaluations have been mainly conducted using small cells. Studies on large cells, especially at the stack level, is still limited \[\]. Since reaction mechanism may change by size of cell, i.e., high CO <sub>2</sub> utilization is difficult to achieve on small size cell, the analysis of reaction mechanism using large size cell is required. Liang et al. obtained different results regarding the contributions of CO₂ electrolysis and RWGS to co-electrolysis when comparing small cells \[\] and large cells \[\]. Analyses have also indicated that as cells size increases, the non-uniformity of gas composition, current, and temperature increases, impacting the reactions and products \[\]. Additionally, while there have been several reports on the results for large single cells and multi-cell stacks, there are very few studies comparing the results of single cells and multi-cell stacks to evaluate the effects of the number of cells \[\]. Therefore, this study aims to elucidate the effects of various parameters on co-electrolysis reactions using a large single cell. Furthermore, 3 cell stack was evaluated to compare the results of the single cell, aiming to clarify the impact of increasing the number of stacked cells on co-electrolysis reactions. These findings are intended to enable control over the composition of gases produced at the stack level in co-electrolysis.

## 2\. Materials and methods

The large cell used for electrolysis was obtained by Elcogen and consisted of porous Ni-YSZ fuel electrode substrate, Ni-YSZ active fuel electrode layer, YSZ electrolyte, GDC barrier layer, and an LSC [oxygen electrode](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/oxygen-electrode). The large cell was an anode supported cell (ASC), with the following layer thicknesses: electrolyte – 3 µm, half-cell (electrolyte + active fuel electrode layer + support layer) – 400 µm, and oxygen electrode – 15 µm. The effective area of the large cell was 25 cm². This large cell was integrated into stack manifolds by MAGNEX and tested for configurations containing one and three cells. illustrates a schematic image of the cell integrated into stack manifolds, representing the single-cell configuration as an example. The large cell is installed between two metallic separators, with air flowing to the oxygen electrode side and [gaseous mixture](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/gaseous-mixture) of CO <sub>2</sub> and H <sub>2</sub> O was flow into the cathode electrode side with a cross-flow direction. The space between the separators is sealed with sealing material, and both ends of the stack are clamped by metallic end plates.

An apparatus capable of evaluating [SOEC](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/electrolysis-cell) co-electrolysis was designed and used. Two systems were developed: one for low flow rates (1–2 cells) and another for high flow rates (3 cells or more). Both systems consisted of a gas supply system, [water vapor](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/materials-science/water-vapor) supply system, [electric furnace](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/electric-furnace), and gas-liquid separator. The large single cell was evaluated using the low-flow apparatus, while the 3 cells stack test was evaluated using the high-flow apparatus. The water vapor supply system varied: the low-flow apparatus employed a [humidifier](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/humidifier) to control water vapor concentration via [humidification](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/humidification) temperature, while the high-flow apparatus used a direct evaporation of feed water to control water vapor concentration via supplied water flow rate. Mixed gases of CO₂ and H₂ from the gas supply system were introduced into the water vapor supply system, where they were mixed with water vapor and supplied to the electric furnace. To reduce the dissolution of CO₂ in water inside the humidifier, the humidifier was ventilated for over an hour under testing conditions (humidification temperature and feed gas composition). The stack manifold inside the furnace was preheated to the test temperature at a rate of 70 °C/h by flowing air to the oxygen electrode and nitrogen to the fuel electrode. The fuel electrode was gradually reduced by varying the H₂/N₂ volume ratio (3/97 → 10/90 → 25/75 → 50/50 → 75/25 → 100/0), holding each condition for at least 30 min. After setting the condition to 100 % H₂, voltage stabilization was monitored for over 30 min to complete reduction. Following reduction, electrolysis was performed using a DC power supply (KIKUSUI PWR401L) connected to the stack manifold, and I-V characteristics were obtained. To prevent cell damage, measurements were performed at voltages below 1.6 V per cell. During large single cell testing, stack temperatures were monitored using a K-type [thermocouple](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/thermocouple), positioned centrally within a hole in the [endplate](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/endplate) (). Gases discharged from the cells were analyzed using a gas chromatograph (GC, Shimadzu GC-2014ATF), which enabled direct water vapor analysis. To prevent water vapor condensation, pipes between the water vapor supply system and furnace, as well as pipes between the furnace and GC, were heated to over 160 °C. The GC utilized two detectors: TCD for detecting H₂, CO, CO₂, and water vapor, and FID for hydrocarbons. Under current test conditions, hydrocarbons were observed below detection limits. During GC measurements, each test condition (temperature, gas composition, current) was maintained for at least 30 min before measurements began. GC measurements were repeated at least two time under identical conditions to confirm steady-state operation.

From, it can be seen that the difference between the measured and the equilibrium concentrations of RWGS was quite small under all conditions, suggesting that the single cell outlet composition is close to the [equilibrium composition](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/equilibrium-composition) with RWGS. Although the measured values tended to be slightly lower than the equilibrium values, the difference between the measured and equilibrium values remained constant regardless of temperature or feed gas composition. Therefore, the difference between the measured value and the equilibrium value is considered to be due to uncertainties in the experimental conditions and measurement procedures, rather than the outlet composition not having reached equilibrium. From these findings, it is considered that the outlet gas composition under all the test conditions in reached the RWGS equilibrium composition. This result supports the idea that in the CO formation pathway, H <sub>2</sub> is first generated by [water vapor](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/materials-science/water-vapor) electrolysis, and then that H <sub>2</sub> reacts with CO₂ in the feed gas through RWGS to form CO. Here it is noted that if CO <sub>2</sub> electrolysis and H <sub>2</sub> O electrolysis occurs at ratio close to the RWGS equilibrium concentration, the measured and theoretical values could match even if CO <sub>2</sub> electrolysis were occurred significantly. However, it is difficult to assume that CO <sub>2</sub> electrolysis and H <sub>2</sub> O electrolysis were occurred at a ratio close to the RWGS equilibrium compositions at all temperatures and the feed gas compositions used in our research. From these results, it can be concluded that the single cell outlet composition under all measured conditions is almost in equilibrium with RWGS, and that the CO formation rate is larger by RWGS than that of the CO <sub>2</sub> electrolysis.

Another factor that may cause a change in the slope of the I-V curve at a specific current density is the shift from endothermic to exothermic behavior at the thermal [neutral voltage](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/neutral-voltage) (V <sub>tn</sub>). V <sub>tn</sub> is the voltage at which the reaction heat and Joule heat are balanced. shows the temperature change of the single cell stack during the electrolysis measurement at 700 °C. The temperature change is shown as the difference of temperature, ΔT, temperature under application of current and that at OCV as the reference. ΔT shows similar behavior in all gas compositions feed, i.e., the temperature decreasing in the [low current density](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/low-current-density) region and increasing in the high current density region. The reason for this temperature dependence with respect to current density is that the amount of heat absorbed by the [endothermic reactions](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/endothermic-reaction), H <sub>2</sub> O electrolysis and CO <sub>2</sub> electrolysis, are proportional to the current density, while the amount of Joule heat generated is proportional to the square of the current density (I <sup>2</sup> R). V <sub>tn</sub> is the voltage at which the amount of heat absorbed and generated is equal, i.e., the temperature is the same as the OCV. One possible reason why the slope of the I-V curve changes at a certain point of the current density is that the heat absorption and heat generation may change at V <sub>tn</sub>. From, the current density at V <sub>tn</sub> is around 0.3A/cm <sup>2</sup> for all feed gas compositions, which corresponds to approximately 1.3 V from the results shown in. On the other hand, in, the current density at which the slope was changed was around 0.2A/cm <sup>2</sup> at 700 °C, so it was not corresponded with that at V <sub>tn</sub>. Therefore, the transition between heat absorption and heat generation at V <sub>tn</sub> may not be the reason for the slope changed in the I-V curve. Other possible reasons for the change in slope was the significant activation [overvoltage](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/overvoltage) in the low current density region and the change in the rate-limiting [resistive component](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/resistive-component) in the high current density region. Therefore, the exact reason for the change in the slope of the I-V curve remains unclear. However, despite the change in slope, the concentration of CO and H₂ exhibited a linear relationship with current density, as shown in. This indicates that the change in slope was not caused by the ratio of CO and H₂. Consequently, this phenomenon does not impact the control of product composition.

Regarding the effect of temperature on the I-V curve, the slope of the I-V curve became smaller as the temperature increased. This temperature dependence is almost the same with the results of the previous studies, which showed that at the higher temperature, the minimum thermodynamically required electrical energy (ΔG) was smaller for both H <sub>2</sub> O and CO <sub>2</sub> electrolysis, and the electrode reaction is accelerated kinetically, which reduces the overpotential \[\]. From the temperature dependence of the outlet gas [composition analysis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/materials-science/chemical-composition-analysis), shows the CO production rate which became higher with increasing temperature based on the calculation of Faraday efficiency. If CO is produced by CO <sub>2</sub> electrolysis, the reaction resistance would be larger than that by the [RWGS reaction](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/water-gas-shift-reaction), resulting in a steeper slope of I-V curve. However, since higher temperatures result in decreased reaction resistance due to thermal activation in both H <sub>2</sub> O and CO <sub>2</sub> electrolysis, the increased resistance associated with the higher CO <sub>2</sub> electrolysis ratio might be offset and cannot be observed. Therefore, it is difficult to separate of CO <sub>2</sub> electrolysis and RWGS from the temperature dependence of the I-V curve.

Additionally, examining the effect of the single cell feed gas composition on the I-V curve in, there was almost no difference in products depending on the feed gas composition at 600–650 °C. At 700–750 °C, there was also no difference depending on the feed composition on the high current density region, however a small change was observed at the low current density region, with the I-V curve slope tending to decrease as the CO <sub>2</sub> ratio in the feed gas decreased. Because the overpotential of CO <sub>2</sub> electrolysis is larger than that of H <sub>2</sub> O electrolysis, the proportion of CO <sub>2</sub> electrolysis cause the steeper the slope of the I-V curve. However, we assume that the effect of the feed gas composition observed on the low current density region at 700–750 °C does not reflect the differences in the ratio of H <sub>2</sub> O and CO <sub>2</sub> electrolysis occurred. This is because, as shown in, the concentration of each gas was changed linearly with respect to current density, so the electrolysis ratio of H <sub>2</sub> O and CO <sub>2</sub> remains constant over all current density. Therefore, the phenomenon where the effect of the feed gas composition appears only on the low current density region cannot be explained. Thus, from the I-V results, we can assume that the effect of the feed gas composition on the ratio of H <sub>2</sub> O and CO <sub>2</sub> electrolysis was not observed within the range of the experimental conditions conducted in this study. On the other hand, in, the ratio of CO and H <sub>2</sub> formed was varied depending on the feed gas CO <sub>2</sub> /H <sub>2</sub> O ratio. In other words, the feed gas composition did not affect the CO₂:H₂O electrolysis ratio but influenced the CO:H₂ production ratio. From these results, it is suggested that in spite of the feed gas composition, H <sub>2</sub> O electrolysis is the main electrolysis reaction, and RWGS by formed H <sub>2</sub> is the main reaction for CO production.

The hypothesis that the primary reaction is H₂O electrolysis and that CO formation occurs through successive RWGS reactions is also supported by the thermal neutral potential, V <sub>tn</sub>. V <sub>tn</sub> can be calculated theoretically by using the formula V <sub>tn</sub> = ΔH / nF \[\]. At 700 °C, the theoretical V <sub>tn</sub> is approximately 1.30 V for H₂O electrolysis and 1.46 V for CO₂ electrolysis \[\]. Since CO₂ electrolysis is more endothermic than H <sub>2</sub> O electrolysis, its theoretical V <sub>tn</sub> is higher than that of H₂O electrolysis. In co-electrolysis, the theoretical V <sub>tn</sub> is estimated by the carbon-to-hydrogen ratio \[\]. The contributions of ΔH for CO and H₂ production are reflected in V <sub>tn</sub> depending on the carbon-to-hydrogen ratio. Regarding the CO production pathway, the ΔH values are equal whether CO is generated via CO₂ electrolysis or through the reaction between CO₂ and H <sub>2</sub>, as determined by Hess's law. Therefore, the total ΔH remains constant regardless of the CO production pathway, and it is not expected to affect V <sub>tn</sub>. Nevertheless, the measured V <sub>tn</sub> was consistently around 1.3 V, which corresponds to the theoretical value for H <sub>2</sub> O single electrolysis, which means negligible CO₂ electrolysis occurs. This suggests that the reactions at the triple-phase boundary of the electrode (electrolysis reactions) are primarily H <sub>2</sub> O electrolysis occurred, while CO production is occurred by the RWGS reaction in the gas phase. Since RWGS is not [electrochemical reaction](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/materials-science/electrochemical-reaction), V <sub>tn</sub> is determined by the simple steam electrolysis reaction. Due to the high feed rate per unit catalyst amount at the electrode, it is difficult for the reaction at the electrode to achieve the equilibrium. Additionally, the [apparent activation energy](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/apparent-activation-energy) of H₂O is much lower than that of CO₂, making H <sub>2</sub> O electrolysis more favorable and indicating that V <sub>tn</sub> is independent of CO₂/H₂O ratio. This effect is particularly pronounced in larger cells, where primary reactions on the electrode was dominated. It is inferred that the primary reaction on the electrode is H <sub>2</sub> O electrolysis, while CO is produced by the RWGS reaction occurring either in the gas phase or during diffusion within the electrode.

The results of our large single cell and 3 cell stack study suggested that the primary reaction for CO production is mainly assigned to RWGS reaction, based on the comparison of the measured stack outlet gas with the equilibrium concentration of the RWGS, and the slope of the I-V curve and V <sub>tn</sub>. Wolf et al. conducted co-electrolysis experiments using small cells with Ni-cermet electrodes at 800 °C, focusing on low H₂O concentration conditions. They found that when the equilibrium H <sub>2</sub> O concentration in the feed gas was lower than 30 %, the ASR in the I-V curve was larger than that of individual H <sub>2</sub> O electrolysis and was dependent on the H <sub>2</sub> O concentration. However, when the equilibrium H <sub>2</sub> O concentration was higher than 30 %, the ASR remains constant independent of the H <sub>2</sub> O concentration, indicating that RWGS becomes the main reaction for CO production \[\]. Similarly, Dittrich et al. conducted similar experiments using small cells with Ni-cermet electrodes at 900 °C and obtained results suggesting that CO₂ electrolysis becomes significant when the equilibrium H₂O concentration is smaller than 20 % \[\]. Our experiments were performed using a stack at 700 °C, which differs from their conditions. However, our experimental conditions also had a relatively high H <sub>2</sub> O concentration of 30 % or higher, which was considered to be the reason why CO was mainly formed by RWGS. So, it is considered that the co-electrolysis reaction mechanism in large cells is similar to that in small cells. It can be assumed that by further decreasing the H <sub>2</sub> O concentration, CO <sub>2</sub> electrolysis will be proceed for CO formation. The results of our 3 cells stack showed similar outlet gas composition and [electrochemical properties](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/materials-science/electrochemical-property) as similar manner with large single cell. This suggests that even when cells are stacked, the main reaction mechanisms and outlet gas composition is not significantly changed, suggesting that the primary reaction for the CO formation is also RWGS and CO <sub>2</sub> electrolysis was hardly occurred on Ni base cathode under CO <sub>2</sub> H <sub>2</sub> O co-electrolysis condition.

## 4\. Conclusions

Co-electrolysis experiments were conducted using large size single cells used for stack and also 3 cells stack. The effects of operating parameters and the number of cells on the composition of the products were examined, and the reaction mechanisms were discussed. In the large size single cell experiment, the effects of various operating parameters (composition of feed gas, flow rate of feed gas, temperature) on the product composition and I-V performance were evaluated. The ratio of produced CO and H <sub>2</sub> was varied depending on the composition of the feed gas and temperature, however the ratio was almost unaffected by the flow rate. Therefore, it was considered that controlling the feed gas composition and temperature would be effective for obtaining the required ratio of CO and H <sub>2</sub>. In the case of changing the temperature, there are issues such as damage to the cell and stack, a decrease in energy efficiency, and the time required for stabilizing the operation, so it was considered that controlling the feed gas composition would be the most effective for controlling products. Within the range of the test conditions used in this study, for example, at 750 °C, it was possible to control the CO production ratio between 0 and 50 % by controlling the composition of the feed gas. The results of both the single cell outlet gas [composition analysis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/materials-science/chemical-composition-analysis) and the I-V performance suggested that the CO <sub>2</sub> electrolysis to CO production is not proceeded and mainly formed by RWGS under all experimental conditions. This suggests that it may be possible to predict and control the product composition based on the [thermodynamic equilibrium](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/thermodynamic-equilibrium) of RWGS. Furthermore, interesting results were obtained from the measurement of the thermal [neutral voltage](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/neutral-voltage) of the large size single cell, suggesting that the locations which means steam electrolysis as a primary reaction dominantly occurred at TPB and following reaction of RWGS occurred in gas phase to form CO. Even when the number of cells was increased to 3 cells stack, the same dependency in outlet gas composition and electrochemical characteristics were observed with the large size single cell. This suggests that even when the cells are stacked, the main reaction mechanisms is not changed, i.e., the primary reaction is steam electrolysis and CO was mainly formed by RWGS.
