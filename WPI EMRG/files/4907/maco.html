Abstract

In the present study, corrosion-protective microarc oxidation (MAO) coatings were prepared on AZ31B, AZ80, and ZK60 cast magnesium alloy substrates in an alkaline silicate electrolyte. The corrosion performances of the uncoated and MAO-coated alloys were investigated using electrochemical and salt spray chamber corrosion tests. The microstructure characterization and experimental results show that among the three alloys studied, the ZK60 Mg alloy exhibited the best and AZ31B the least corrosion resistance under the salt spray conditions. The MAO coating provided robust corrosion protection of the Mg substrates and resulted in a significant decrease in the corrosion rate of the alloys by 3–4 orders of magnitude. The MAO coating on ZK60 alloy showed better corrosion protectiveness than that on the AZ series alloys due to the incorporation of different alloying elements in the coating, especially the Zn and Al elements, which are from the Mg substrate. The corrosion performances and mechanisms of the uncoated and MAO-coated Mg alloys are interpreted in terms of the microstructure and phase/chemical compositions of both the substrates and coatings.

1 INTRODUCTION

The ever-increasing energy demand worldwide and strict environmental and government regulations have stimulated the development of lightweight vehicles in the past few decades. Mg and its alloys, as the lightest metallic materials, are receiving more and more attention for manufacturing lightweight structural components,1-3 due to their unique combination of properties, including excellent mechanical properties, low density, high specific strength, and stiffness.3 Yet, presently, Mg alloys are still not as widely used as Al or steel alloys in the automotive industry. A major limitation is the inferior corrosion properties of Mg alloys when exposed to humid environments, especially those containing aggressive electrolyte species, such as chloride ions.4

Recently, intensive research and development work has been conducted in both academic and industrial institutes to develop advanced materials processing and surface treatment technologies for replacing aluminum using Mg alloys in the design and manufacture of fatigue-critical automotive components, such as the control arm.5-7 Three grades of Mg alloy (i.e., AZ31B, AZ80, and ZK60) were selected as the candidate materials for the development of cost-effective forging processes for the fabrication of a lower control arm,8 due to their superior mechanical properties and excellent forgeability.7, 9, 10 The corrosion properties of the bare Mg alloys substrate are determined by factors such as the alloy elements, phase composition, microstructure, processing history, and surface finish and so on.4 For the majority of Mg–Al alloys, aluminum is partly in the solid solution and partly precipitated in the form of intermetallic compounds like Mg17Al12 (β-phase), which is electrochemically cathodic to the α-Mg matrix and whose volume fraction, size, and distribution influence the corrosion behavior of the alloys significantly.4, 11, 12 The ZK family of Mg alloys contains zinc and zirconium and has good strength and formability.8, 13 In the ZK series alloys, the elements Zn and Zr also play an important role in the formation and refinement of grains, secondary phases, and surface films, which, in turn, influence the corrosion behavior of the ZK60 alloy.14-16

In automotive applications, the external components and structures are often exposed to humid environments containing aggressive species such as road salt, raising concerns over the corrosion of bare Mg alloys. An effective approach to mitigate corrosion is to coat Mg alloys to prevent contact with the corrosive environment. The previous works17, 18 in our group have shown that the cold-sprayed pure aluminum and AA7075 alloy coatings can provide excellent corrosion protection of AZ31B cast alloy in the salt spray environment. However, the cold spray process requires a specialized coating facility and is difficult to apply on substrates of complex shapes. Among the various coating technologies for treating magnesium alloys, microarc oxidation (MAO), developed from conventional anodic oxidation, has commonly been applied for years to form a hard ceramic coating on Al, Ti, Mg, and their alloys.19, 20 The MAO process can be used to treat parts of complex shapes and it produces outstanding in situ-grown ceramic-like oxide coatings on magnesium alloys, which are thick, hard, and adhesive to the substrate with high corrosion resistance.21 Substantial research has been carried out to study the corrosion performances of MAO-coated Mg alloys. Most of the investigations focused on the MAO mechanisms and optimization of the processing parameters such as current density, process time, and electrolyte composition and so on, as well as their effect on the electrochemical corrosion behavior of the materials.22-27 It was reported that the microstructure (i.e., roughness, porosity, and thickness) and phase composition of the MAO coating play a critical role in influencing the corrosion resistance.28, 29 Cakmak et al.30 studied the corrosion properties of the MAO coating on different Mg substrates. They found that the MAO-coated AZ series alloys have a better corrosion resistance than the MAO-coated AM series alloys. However, so far, little has been done to understand the effects of the contents of Al and Zn, as the most important alloying elements in Mg alloy, on the corrosion behavior and corrosion mechanism of the bare and MAO-coated Mg alloys under salt environments.

In the present work, the corrosion properties of as-cast AZ31B, AZ80, and ZK60 alloys with and without MAO coatings have been investigated using advanced electrochemical and salt spray testing methods. The cast Mg alloys were used as the starting materials for development of the lower control arm.6, 10, 31 On the basis of the results of the corrosion properties of cast alloys, the corrosion and corrosion fatigue performances of the forged Mg alloys with and without coatings will be investigated in the follow-up experiments. In this study, the effects of the main alloying elements, especially Al and Zn, on the chemical, phase compositions, and corrosion protectiveness of the bare and MAO coatings formed on different grades of Mg–Al and Mg–Zn alloys were characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometry (EDS), X-ray diffraction (XRD), and corrosion techniques. The corrosion mechanisms of different grades of uncoated and MAO-coated Mg alloys are discussed in correlation with the microstructure and composition of the substrate and MAO coating.

2 EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURE

2.1 Materials

The materials used in this investigation were commercially available AZ31B, AZ80, and ZK60 magnesium alloys. These alloys were received in the as-cast condition and their nominal chemical compositions are given in Table 1.32

Table 1. Chemical compositions of as-cast AZ31B, AZ80, and ZK60 magnesium alloys (wt%)
Alloys Al Zn Zr Mn Si Cu Mg
AZ31B 3.1 1.05 – 0.54 0.02 0.0008 Balance
AZ80 8.4 0.48 – 0.2 0.026 0.0026 Balance
ZK60 – 5.2 0.47 0.09 0.03 0.005 Balance

The alloys were mechanically cut into flat coupons with the dimensions of 50 × 25 × 3 mm. Before the MAO treatment and corrosion tests, all the specimens were ground with SiC abrasive papers up to 1,200 grit to ensure the same surface roughness. Subsequently, the specimens were ultrasonically degreased in acetone, rinsed with ethanol, and then blew dry at room temperature.

2.2 MAO processing

Cast Mg coupons were treated in a silicate alkaline aqueous electrolyte, including Na2SiO3, KF, and KOH to obtain MAO coatings. The customized power supply for MAO processing with single pulse direct current was self-designed and self-manufactured. During the MAO treatment, the Mg alloy coupon and the stainless steel container were used as the anode and the cathode, respectively. The MAO process was carried out at a constant current of 1 A (corresponding to a constant current density of 34 mA/cm2 for the Mg alloy coupons) and a pulse width of 80 μs for 5 min, which are the optimized parameters in the previous work.33 The temperature of the electrolyte was kept at 30°C by a cooling system. The pH of the silicate electrolyte solution was adjusted to 13 using KOH solution. After the MAO treatment, the specimens were first cleaned ultrasonically in trichloroethylene, rinsed with distilled water, and then blew dry and stored in a desiccator for further analysis.

2.3 Microstructure analysis of the base alloys and MAO coatings

For metallographic characterization of the base alloys, the uncoated specimens were wet ground through successive grades of silicon carbide abrasive papers from P120 to P2000. The etching reagent used was an acetic-picral solution containing 10 ml acetic acid glacial, 4.2 g picric acid, 10 ml H2O, and 70 ml ethanol (95%). After etching, the specimens were washed in a stream of alcohol and then dried with a blast of air.

The surface and cross-section morphology and elemental compositions of the MAO coatings on different Mg alloy substrates were investigated using a scanning electron microscope NOVA NanoSEM 650 equipped with EDS. The phase composition of the coatings was identified by the XRD technique using Cu-Kα radiation with a Bruker-D8 discover 2D diffractometer.

2.4 Electrochemical testing

The corrosion performances of Mg alloys were studied using electrochemical techniques. Potentiodynamic polarization curves for the uncoated and MAO-coated specimens were obtained using a Solartron Analytical 1287A potentiostat/galvanostat controlled by a computer at room temperature in a corrosive medium, 3.5 wt% NaCl solution. The specimens were tested using a standard three-electrode cell setup with a Pt gauze as the counter electrode, Ag/AgCl (sat. KCl) as the reference, and the bare and MAO-coated Mg alloys (with an exposed area of 1 cm2) as the working electrode. The potential of the working electrode was scanned with respect to the open-current potential at a sweep rate of 1 mV/s, and then a Tafel fit of the potentiodynamic curve was conducted using software developed in house. At least three repetitive specimens were tested to ensure good reproducibility. The typical results of each specimen are presented in this paper.

2.5 Continuous salt spray corrosion test

To further characterize the long-term corrosion process and corrosion performances of the different Mg alloys, the uncoated and MAO-coated specimens were put in a Singleton Corp. SCCH-Salt Spray Chamber and exposed to a static environment of continuous salt spray of a 5 wt% NaCl solution for 720 hr (30 days) at 35°C and 100% relative humidity according to ASTM B117 standard.34 At certain time points (4, 6, 9, 11, 14, 17, 23, and 30 days), at least two repetitive samples of each alloy were extracted from the corrosion testing chamber for weight loss measurement and surface analysis by SEM, EDS, and optical imaging.

Before the weight loss measurement, the specimens were cleaned as per ASTM G1–0335 to remove the corrosion products. The aqueous cleaning solution consisted of chromium trioxide (CrO3, 200 g/L), silver nitrate (AgNO3, 10 g/L), and barium nitrate (Ba(NO3)2, 20 g/L). The mass changes and mass loss ratio after exposure to the salt spray corrosion testing were recorded for each specimen. In all cases, tests were performed in duplicate to guarantee the reliability of the results, and the mass loss value for each time point is an average of the duplicated specimens. Then the distinct breakdown time and morphology of the MAO coatings on different Mg alloys were analyzed by SEM and optical microscopy.

3 RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

3.1 Microstructure characterization

Figure 1 shows the optical micrographs of the microstructure of AZ31B, AZ80, and ZK60 cast Mg alloys. It can be seen that all three alloys have α-Mg matrix embedded with particles of secondary phases in general. The AZ series alloys mainly contain the Mg–Al matrix and Mg17Al12 secondary phase. For an Al-containing Mg alloy, the Mg–Al solid solution matrix becomes less active as the Al content increases.36 Lunder et al.37 have observed that when the content of aluminum reaches 8% (mass fraction), the corrosion resistance of magnesium alloy could be greatly improved.12 Thus, the AZ80 alloy is expected to be more corrosion-resistant than the AZ31B alloy. Moreover, the amount and distribution of the secondary phase in Mg alloys also influence their corrosion performances. In the AZ31B alloy (Figure 1a), there are only discrete β-phase (Mg17Al12) particles, while in the AZ80 alloy (Figure 1b), because of the higher aluminum content, a much higher volume fraction of β-Mg17Al12 intermetallic compound is present, forming a nearly continuous network, especially along the grain boundary. The β-Mg17Al12 phase is nobler than the α-Mg matrix and thus can act as a microgalvanic cathode to accelerate the corrosion of the matrix,4 when the amount of the secondary phase is small and its distribution is discontinuous, like in the AZ31B alloy. However, the stable secondary phase can also retard the corrosion of the alloy through a barrier mechanism,12 when its volume fraction is large enough and a continuous network of the intermetallic compound is formed, like in the AZ80 alloy.

In the ZK60 alloy, the secondary phase observed is β-MgZn2 (Figure 1c), which is even nobler than the Mg17Al12.38 Due to the high content of Zn, a network of MgZn2 secondary phase similar to that in the AZ80 alloy was formed. Therefore, a stronger barrier effect from the secondary phase and better corrosion resistance are expected for the ZK60 alloy. Moreover, fewer defects such as micropores and inclusions generated during the casting process are seen in the ZK60 alloy compared with the AZ31B and AZ80 alloys, which is likely attributed to the purifying effect of Zr element in ZK60.39

The corrosion performances of the three Mg alloys were characterized using a potentiodynamic polarization technique and will be discussed later in comparison with the MAO-coated Mg alloys. In this study, corrosion-protective MAO coatings were deposited on all three Mg alloy substrates. The SEM images of the typical morphologies of the MAO-coated Mg alloys are shown in Figure 2. The surface of the MAO-treated magnesium alloys in Figures 2a, 2c, and 2e featured a lot of volcano top-like micropores with a diameter of about 1 μm. The pores were formed by the molten metal and eruption of gas bubbles in the microdischarging period during MAO process.40, 41 There is no obvious difference in the surface morphology of these MAO-coated Mg alloy specimens.

To measure the thickness of the MAO coatings on different Mg alloy substrates, the coated specimens were cross-sectioned and mounted in epoxy resin for metallographic and SEM study. Figures 2b, 2d, and 2f shows the cross-sectional SEM images and EDS analysis data of different regions of the MAO coatings on AZ31B, AZ80, and ZK60, respectively. It can be seen that dense and uniform ceramic coatings with strong adhesion to the substrate are formed on the magnesium alloys. The average thickness of the MAO coatings on all three alloys is the same, about 5.5 μm. Although micropores and microcracks can still be found in the outer layer of the coatings, a continuous compact inner layer was formed at the metal/coating interface, which prevents the penetration of corrosive medium through the surface pores and cracks and provides robust protection to the Mg alloy substrate underneath. EDS was used to identify differences in chemical composition between the inner and outer layers of the MAO coatings. The EDS data showed that the MAO coating mainly consists of O, F, Mg, Al, and Si for AZ31B and AZ80 alloys, and O, F, Mg, Zn, and Si for the ZK60 alloy. These elements are derived from both the substrate and electrolyte for MAO treatment.

EDS elemental mapping analysis was conducted on the cross-section specimens of the MAO-coated alloys and the data are shown in Figure 3. As the MAO coatings consist of magnesium oxide, fluoride, and silicate, the mapping revealed the presence of Mg, O, F, and Si elements for all MAO coatings on the three alloys. Combining the EDS data of Figures 2b, 2d, and 2f, it is shown that the inner MAO coating layer near the coating/substrate interface contains a lower oxygen content and higher fluorine content than the outer coating layer for all three Mg alloys. For Al-containing AZ31B and AZ80 alloys, Al was mainly in the outer layer of their MAO coating, with AZ80 showing a slightly higher Al content in its coating. In the ZK60 alloy, Zn, instead of Al, was seen in the MAO coating, derived from the reactions of the alloy substrate with the silicate electrolyte during the MAO processing, and the inner compact layer had a slightly higher Zn concentration than the outer layer.

To further ascertain the phase composition of the MAO coatings obtained in the Mg alloys, the bare and coated specimens were analyzed using the XRD technique. Figure 4 gives the XRD data for the MAO-coated Mg alloys in comparison with those for the bare substrates. The AZ31B, AZ80, and ZK60 Mg coupons were found to consist of primarily α-phase (α-Mg) and various secondary β-phases, which are consistent with the findings of the metallographic study (Figure 1). For AZ31B, because a low volume fraction of the secondary phase as can be seen from the metallographic analysis (Figure 1a), no characteristic peaks of the β-Mg17Al12 phase were obtained besides the Mg peaks in the XRD pattern. For AZ80, peaks of the β-Mg17Al12 phase are seen due to its much higher volume fraction (Figure 1b). For ZK60, the secondary phases identified are MgZn2 and Zn2Zr intermetallic compounds, which is consistent with what was reported in literature.39 As the Zr content in ZK60 is relatively low (<1%), the volume fraction of the Zn2Zr compound is much smaller than that of the MgZn2 phase, and only MgZn2 particles were seen in the metallographic image (Figure 1c).

The XRD patterns for coated Mg alloys showed characteristic peaks from both the Mg substrate and the ceramic compounds in the MAO coating, including magnesium oxide (MgO), magnesium fluoride (MgF2), magnesium silicate (Mg2SiO4), and the complex silicate compounds Mg3Al2(SiO4)3, and MgxFy(SiO4)z. These compounds are commonly seen in MAO coatings on Mg alloys treated in silicate solution.30, 42, 43 No significant difference in main phase species of the MAO coating is seen, but the contents of the various ceramic compounds may be different for the three Mg alloys investigated and affect the corrosion performances.

3.2 Electrochemical testing

The corrosion properties of the bare and MAO-coated Mg alloys were studied using the potentiodynamic polarization testing technique. The typical polarization curves obtained for the MAO-coated and bare Mg alloys are plotted in Figure 5. The Tafel fitting results of these curves and the corresponding electrochemical parameters of these curves are listed in Table 2, where Ecorr is the corrosion potential, icorr the corrosion current density, ba the anodic Tafel constant, bc the cathodic Tafel constant, and Rp the polarization resistance. The values of these parameters can be obtained by fitting the potentiodynamic curve in the weak polarization zone within the range from ±10 to ±50 mV away from Ecorr.44

From the polarization curves, it is clearly seen that the MAO coating provided significant protection to the Mg alloy substrate against corrosion in the salt solution, as all the coated specimens showed icorr values 3–4 orders of magnitude lower than those for the corresponding uncoated Mg alloys. The Ecorr values for the MAO-coated specimens were about 250 mV nobler than those for the uncoated Mg alloys (Table 2). Among all the MAO-coated Mg alloys, the coated ZK60 alloy specimen exhibited the lowest icorr, largest Rp, and highest Ecorr. The higher ba values for the MAO-coated specimens than those for the uncoated Mg alloys indicate that for the coated specimens, the corrosion current density changes slowly with the potential during the anodic reaction process.4 The anodic reactions have a close relationship with the surface state of the metal. When an MAO coating is formed on the Mg substrate, the passage of the metal ions through the MAO coating takes place very slowly and thus the corrosion current resulting from the movement of the metal ions leaving the metal becomes very small. In other words, the MAO coating exhibited a suppressing effect on the anodic reactions of the Mg alloys, leading to a markedly improved corrosion resistance of the coated specimens than the bare substrates. The fact that the ba values of the MAO-coated specimens are higher than the theoretic value 118 mV/decade for one-electron oxidation indicates that the anodic reactions are the rate-determining process.45

From the potentiodynamic polarization curves, a corrosion rate 3–4 orders of magnitude lower than that for the bare substrate is predicated for the MAO-coated Mg alloys (Table 2), with MAO-coated ZK60 showing the lowest corrosion rate. To study the long-term corrosion performances of the Mg alloys, bare, and MAO-coated AZ31B, AZ80, and ZK60 alloy coupons were exposed to continuous salt spray testing for up to 30 days.

3.3 Continuous salt spray corrosion test

3.3.1 Mass loss measurement

After exposure to the continuous salt spray of 5wt% NaCl solution as per the ASTM B117 standard34 for the desired time duration, coupons of the bare and MAO-coated Mg alloys were taken out of the chamber and measured for mass loss after removing the corrosion products. Figure 6 shows the variation in mass loss with exposure time for all bare and coated Mg alloys. To further demonstrate the corrosion severity for different alloys, the mass loss ratio calculated as the percentage of the total mass loss with respect to the original coupon mass before testing was also plotted. Before the salt spray exposure, the bare Mg alloys had a shiny and smooth surface. After the salt spray test, corroded dark areas appeared on the specimens, resulting in increased the surface roughness and loss of the shiny reflective metallic appearance. With time, corrosion products in the form of white powders accumulated on the metal surface. The variation of mass loss with time for the three bare alloys (AZ31B, AZ80, and ZK60) is shown in Figure 6a, from which it can be clearly seen that corrosion occurred immediately after the start of the salt spray exposure. The mass loss for all three alloys increased almost linearly with time until around the 20th day, after which time point, the corrosion process gradually slowed down, likely due to the covering of the metal surfaces by excessive corrosion products. The monotonous increase of mass loss for all three alloys in the first 20 days of the salt spray test can be ascribed to the nonprotective, porous, and inadherent surface film formed on Mg alloys.46, 47

The slope of the mass loss versus time curve gives the corrosion rate of the alloy. A larger slope indicates a higher corrosion rate. The AZ31B alloy exhibited poor corrosion resistance and degraded rapidly. Images of the AZ31B specimen on the 23rd day and AZ80 and ZK60 alloys on the 30th day after removing the corrosion products are also shown in Figure 6a. The AZ80 alloy corroded slower than AZ31B, and less mass loss after the same time span was seen for AZ80. ZK60 exhibited the best corrosion resistance among the three Mg alloys. After 30 days of aggressive continuous salt spray exposure, about 50 wt% of the ZK60 specimen remained free of corrosion. In short, the corrosion rate of the base Mg alloys in the salt spray decreases in the order of AZ31B > AZ80 > ZK60, which is in good agreement with the results of the microstructure analysis and electrochemical testing.

Figure 6b shows the variation in mass loss with time for the MAO-coated Mg alloy specimens. Images of the MAO-coated coupons after 30 days of salt spray corrosion test with the corrosion products removed are shown in Figure 7. Compared with the bare substrates, significantly less mass loss was seen for the MAO-coated specimens, verifying the robust protectiveness of the MAO coating against corrosion. It is worth noting that only slight localized corrosion occurred at the edge of the MAO-coated ZK60 alloy coupon (Figure 7c), and most of the coated area remained intact after 30 days. Almost no mass loss was measured for MAO-coated ZK60 in contrast to the ~50% mass loss for bare ZK60 on the 30th day of salt spray exposure.

3.3.2 Corrosion characterization of as-cast AZ80 and ZK60 alloys

In general, the corrosion resistance of the different series of Mg–Al and Mg–Zn alloys has a direct relationship with the concentration of alloying elements and distribution of the secondary β-phase. The secondary phases containing a binary alloy of Mg17Al12 and MgZn2 have nobler potential than the Mg matrix, and they can act as a cathode and the Mg matrix as an anode at the interface between them, resulting in galvanic corrosion. Figure 8 shows the surface corrosion morphologies observed by SEM of as-cast AZ80 and ZK60 in continuous salt spray environment for 4 hr and the corresponding EDS element mapping analysis. It can be seen that irregular corrosion pits with large size are present in Figure 8a, and the pitting corrosion occurs (Figure 8b) on the surface of the AZ80 alloy. According to the corresponding EDS mapping (Figure 8c), the pitting corrosion is mainly caused by the microgalvanic cell composed of the α-Mg matrix and Al–Mn particle. The at% concentrations of this Al–Mn obtained from the EDS results are 6.63% for Mg, 1.45% for O, 53.48% for Al, and 38.44% for Mn. The Al/Mn ratio of this particle is 1.39, which approximates the stoichiometric ratio of 1.6 for Al8Mn5 intermetallic compounds in Mg alloys. This observation agrees with those reported by other researchers.48, 49 As shown in Figure 8d, the ZK60 alloy mainly exhibited filiform corrosion. Combining with the back-scattered electron (BSE) images (Figure 8e), the corrosion filament along grain boundaries forming the trench-like surface is visible on the ZK60 alloy. It was clear that the loose corrosion product (magnesium oxide obtained from the EDS result in Figure 8f) was located in the vicinity of secondary phases and it protruded from the substrate surface. With increasing corrosion time for 24 hr, it is shown in Figure 9a that the corrosion further deteriorates in view of the existence of localized corrosion on the entire surface of AZ80. For the ZK60 alloy, the filiform corrosion further aggravated, together with the presence of pitting corrosion, as shown in Figure 9b. The surface corrosion states of ZK60 are not as severe as that of AZ80, indicating that less preferential corrosion attacks caused by cathodic particles were present in the microstructure.

3.3.3 Protectiveness of MAO coatings on different Mg alloys

For the MAO-coated Mg alloy specimens, the uniform hard ceramic coating acted as a barrier layer between the Mg alloy substrate and salt corrosive environment. It is worth noticing that in the salt spray test, remarkably different corrosion initiation and coating breakdown time were observed for the three Mg alloys, despite the fact that the MAO coatings on the different Mg alloys are of similar microstructure and thickness. As shown in the inset of Figure 6b and Figures 10-12, the MAO-coated AZ31B started to show signs of pitting and coating breakdown from the 4th day. The photograph and SEM images of the MAO-coated AZ31B taken after 4 days in continuous salt spray environment are given in Figure 10. After 4 days, the MAO-coated AZ31B alloy was damaged, whereas the coating underwent severe pitting corrosion and the corrosion medium reached to the substrate (Figure 10a, area b). An even more enlarged view is shown in Figure 10b in which the surface is covered with corrosion products. In the meantime, many big particles appear on the surface of the MAO coating (Figure 10a, area c and the higher magnification Figure 10c). Figure 10d shows the particle morphology corresponding to Figure 10c (area d). It can be observed that many new microcracks appeared on the particle, and the elemental analysis of area (e) shows that the particle is mainly composed of the elements O and Mg. Compared with the coating before corrosion, the content of O element is increased. This is attributed to degradation of the magnesium oxide and converted into magnesium hydroxide during the corrosion process. Because the molar volume of Mg(OH)2 is larger than that of MgO,50 these new cracks would be induced by the volume expansion. With the further development of the corrosion processing, these particles become more and more loose, and the aggressive ions Cl− will go down to the substrate and accelerate the pitting and local corrosion.

The surface morphologies of MAO-coated AZ80 and ZK60 alloys in the same atmosphere for 4 days are illustrated in the Figure 11. The entire surface of the MAO-coated AZ80 and ZK60 revealed no pitting corrosion (Figures 11a and 11c). For MAO-coated AZ80 too, many particles distributed on its surface (Figure 11b). These particles are similar to those produced on the MAO-coated AZ31B specimen (Figure 10c,d). They are also a mixture of magnesium oxide and magnesium hydroxide, which are the weak areas and increase the possibility of occurrence of pitting corrosion. In the MAO-coated ZK60, the morphology of its surface has no substantial changes before and after corrosion, nor did similar particles appear.

While the MAO-coated AZ80 and ZK60 started to show occurrence of localized corrosion that penetrated the coating and attacked the Mg substrate only after 6 and 14 days, respectively. Figure 12 presents the surface appearances of the MAO-coated AZ80 and ZK60 alloys that began to break down. After 6 days, the MAO-coated AZ80 revealed a corrosion pit on its surface (Figure 12a) with the number of corrosion products and deep holes (Figure 12b), which were possibly associated with the further corrosion of particles that appeared at the early stage of the corrosion. The MAO-coated ZK60 alloy showed less deterioration in Figure 12c with a small pit on its surface after 14 days (Figure 12d). Some solid particles were also produced on the surface of MAO-coated ZK60 (Figure 12e), which appears to be more compact than those formed on MAO-coated AZ31B and AZ80 alloys. The corresponding X-ray elemental analysis of the framed region in area (f) shows that it contains high fluoride and silicate and has no obvious cracks on this particle.

With the progression of coating breakdown, the corrosive salt spray medium gained more and more access to the Mg alloy substrate and the effect of the intrinsic corrosion properties of different Mg alloys started to kick in, which would gradually become the dominating factor determining the corrosion rate of the MAO-coated specimens after 11 days (Figure 6b). Nevertheless, unlike AZ31B and AZ80 alloys, the MAO coating on ZK60 alloy maintained its integrity very well and did not undergo large-area breakdown throughout the 30 days (Figure 7c). For MAO coatings, the protectiveness resulted from the constituents such as Mg2SiO4 and MgF2 phases and the elements from the substrate that are chemically stable.50 MgF2 is considered to be beneficial for corrosion resistance because its solubility is lower than that of magnesium oxide or hydroxide.43 In this study, the alkaline silicate electrolyte used for the coating synthesis promotes the formation of Mg2SiO4 and MgF2 compounds. It has been found that the sodium silicate and potassium fluoride added in the electrolyte participate in the reactions of MAO coating formation24, 51 and can effectively improve the corrosion resistance of the MAO-treated Mg alloy.

3.4 Corrosion mechanisms of bare and MAO-treated Mg alloys

Based on the results of the microstructure analysis and the corrosion processes, the corrosion patterns of the bare and MAO-treated Mg alloys are suggested as follows (Figure 13). The Mg substrates exhibit mainly localized corrosion and the galvanic couples formed by the second phase particles (cathode) and the matrix α-Mg (anode) are the main source of the localized corrosion of magnesium alloy. The corrosion resistance of the different magnesium alloys has a direct relationship with the concentration of alloying elements and the microstructure of the magnesium alloy. The preferential attack on the AZ alloys is attributed to the intermetallic compounds Al8Mn5 and the secondary β-Mg17Al12 (as shown in Figure 13a) for the AZ80 alloy. The galvanic couples formed by Al8Mn5 and matrix are nobler than the secondary β-Mg17Al12 in AZ alloys. The size and distribution of the secondary β-Mg17Al12 play a crucial role in pitting corrosion and intergranular corrosion. As a result, the corrosion morphology of AZ80 is less severe than AZ31B. This phenomenon is attributed to a higher β-Mg17Al12 and more continuous β-Mg17Al12 neighboring finer α-grains. The Zr element in ZK60 alloy can refine grain and purify the composition of the alloy, which results in the good corrosion resistance of ZK60 alloy.16 And the Zn as an alloying element in ZK60-formed β-MgZn2 is more stable than that of β-Mg17Al12 in AZ80 alloy.16, 52, 53 Therefore, the ZK60 alloy exhibits a better corrosion resistance than AZ80 and AZ31B alloys.

For the MAO-treated Mg alloys, no significant difference was found in the surface morphology, porosity, and thickness of the MAO coatings formed on these three different Mg alloys because of the same formation process. However, electrochemical testing data and the corrosion performance after long-term salt spray test have shown that the MAO-coated ZK60 exhibited the highest corrosion resistance and MAO-coated AZ31B showed the least corrosion resistance. This is likely closely related to the intrinsic corrosion properties of the bare Mg alloy substrates, which showed a ranking of corrosion resistance as ZK60 > AZ80 > AZ31B clearly in the continuous salt spray test (Figure 6a). Another factor that may result in the different corrosion performances of the MAO-coated specimens is the possibly distinct contents of the ceramic compound phases in the MAO coating and dissolved substrate elements (Al and Zn) in the ceramic crystal structure that cannot be detected as a separate phase by XRD (Figure 13c,d). According to the results of EDS elemental composition and mapping analysis, the higher atomic concentration of F was observed in the inner layer of the MAO coating on the three Mg alloys as shown in Figure 13c,d. It indicates that the continuous compact inner layer with a higher fluoride content, rather than the porous outer layer, plays a crucial role endowing protectiveness of the Mg alloy substrate against corrosion. The Al element can exist as the Mg3Al2(SiO4)3 phase in the porous outer layer, amorphous compound, or dissolved element in the ceramic crystal structure (Figure 13c). Al is known to a form very dense and tenacious passive film that is very protective. Therefore, the coated AZ80 alloy with a higher Al content in the substrate and thence a slightly higher Al content in the MAO surface coating exhibited a better corrosion resistance than the MAO-coated AZ31B. For the MAO-coated ZK60 alloy, the outstanding corrosion resistance of the MAO-coated ZK60 alloy can be likely attributed to the enrichment of Zn element in the coating, especially in the compact inner layer, which is more stable in Mg alloy than Al (seen in Figure 13d). Hence, the Zn element from the ZK60 substrate played a role in the formation of the MAO coating, presenting excellent protectiveness as compared with the Al element from the AZ series Mg alloys. And the corrosion performances of MAO-coated magnesium alloys are closely correlated with the elemental content of the Mg substrate.

4 CONCLUSIONS

Here, the basic corrosion performances of uncoated and MAO-coated AZ31B, AZ80, and ZK60 alloys were revealed and could be summarized as follows:
  • (1)

    The higher Al content in AZ80 alloy resulted in a higher stability than the AZ31B alloy and the formation of a continuous network of Mg17Al12 β-phase that acts as a barrier retarding corrosion. A similar continuous network of secondary phase MgZn2 in ZK60 alloy that is even better than Mg17Al12 provided excellent corrosion resistance superior to AZ80.

  • (2)

    The MAO coatings provided robust protection of different Mg alloys in corrosion medium as compared with bare Mg alloys. The MAO coating on ZK60 was the most corrosion-resistant and exhibited superior anticorrosion performances than on AZ series alloys largely due to the influence of substrate element composition on the MAO coating formation. The enrichment of Zn in the MAO-coated ZK60, especially in the compact inner layer, is more stable than Al formed in the porous outer layer of the MAO-coated AZ series alloys.
